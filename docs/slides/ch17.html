Recap: Short-Run and Long-Run Aggregate Supply

Recap: SRAS versus LRAS (Chapter 12)

Chapter 12 introduced two different aggregate supply curves, and the difference between them is the entire subject of this chapter.

  • Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS): upward sloping. Input prices, especially nominal wages, are sticky in the short run, so a higher price level raises firms’ revenue faster than it raises their costs, and firms respond by producing more.
  • Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS): vertical, at potential GDP (also called full-employment GDP). Once enough time passes for wages and other input prices to fully adjust, the price level no longer affects how much the economy actually produces.

Chapter 12 built both curves. This chapter answers the question it left open: how, exactly, does the economy get from a short-run equilibrium back to the long-run one?

The Self-Correction Mechanism

Two Ways an Economy Can Miss Potential GDP

Recall from Chapter 12 that aggregate demand (AD) can push the short-run equilibrium away from potential GDP in either direction.

  • An inflationary gap: AD is high enough that short-run equilibrium real GDP sits above potential GDP.
  • A recessionary gap: AD is low enough that short-run equilibrium real GDP sits below potential GDP.

Both gaps are temporary. The economy has a built-in mechanism that eventually closes each one, without any new policy action at all.

Closing an Inflationary Gap

Suppose an increase in aggregate demand pushes real GDP temporarily above potential GDP.

  1. Producing above potential GDP means firms are competing hard for scarce workers, unemployment is unusually low.
  2. That tight labor market eventually pushes nominal wages up, as firms compete for workers.
  3. Higher wages raise firms’ costs, shifting SRAS to the left.
  4. This continues until the economy returns to producing at potential GDP, but now at a permanently higher price level.

This is the self-correction mechanism: the economy’s automatic tendency to return to potential GDP through wage adjustment, with no policy action required.

Closing a Recessionary Gap

The same mechanism runs in reverse when aggregate demand falls and real GDP drops below potential GDP.

  1. Producing below potential GDP means high unemployment: many workers looking for jobs.
  2. High unemployment eventually pushes nominal wages down, but this happens slowly, since wages are sticky downward (recall Chapter 6: firms resist cutting pay, workers resist accepting it).
  3. Falling wages lower firms’ costs, shifting SRAS to the right.
  4. This continues until the economy returns to producing at potential GDP, but now at a lower price level.

The direction is symmetric with the inflationary case, but the speed is not. Downward wage stickiness is exactly why a recessionary self-correction tends to take much longer than an inflationary one.

Example: Self-Correction After an Inflationary Gap

The economy’s aggregate demand curve is fixed at \(P = 14 - 0.2Y\), where \(P\) is the price level (index number) and \(Y\) is real GDP (billions of dollars, illustrative).

Potential GDP is \(Y = 20\). The initial short-run aggregate supply curve is \(SRAS_1: P = 0.3Y + 3\).

  1. Find the initial short-run equilibrium (where \(AD = SRAS_1\)). Is this an inflationary or a recessionary gap?
  2. As wages adjust, \(SRAS\) shifts to \(SRAS_2: P = 0.3Y + 4\). Find the new equilibrium.
  3. Is the new equilibrium at potential GDP? What happened to the price level?

Solution: Self-Correction After an Inflationary Gap

  1. Set \(14 - 0.2Y = 0.3Y + 3\): \(11 = 0.5Y\), so \(Y = 22\), \(P = 14 - 0.2(22) = 9.6\). Since \(22 > 20\) (potential GDP), this is an inflationary gap.
  1. Set \(14 - 0.2Y = 0.3Y + 4\): \(10 = 0.5Y\), so \(Y = 20\), \(P = 14 - 0.2(20) = 10.0\).
  1. Yes, the new equilibrium is exactly at potential GDP (\(Y = 20\)). The price level rose from \(9.6\) to \(10.0\). Real GDP fell back to potential and the price level ended up permanently higher, exactly the self-correction pattern for an inflationary gap.

Harder Problem: Self-Correction After a Recessionary Gap

Aggregate demand is fixed at \(P = 10 - 0.2Y\). Potential GDP is still \(Y = 20\). The initial short-run aggregate supply curve is \(SRAS_1: P = 0.3Y + 1\).

  1. Find the initial short-run equilibrium. Is this a recessionary or an inflationary gap?
  2. As wages adjust downward, \(SRAS\) shifts to \(SRAS_2: P = 0.3Y + 0.5\). Find the new equilibrium.
  3. What happened to the price level this time, and why might this adjustment take longer in practice than the inflationary-gap case?

Review: Self-Correction After a Recessionary Gap

  1. Set \(10 - 0.2Y = 0.3Y + 1\): \(9 = 0.5Y\), so \(Y = 18\), \(P = 10 - 0.2(18) = 6.4\). Since \(18 < 20\), this is a recessionary gap.
  1. Set \(10 - 0.2Y = 0.3Y + 0.5\): \(9.5 = 0.5Y\), so \(Y = 19\), \(P = 10 - 0.2(19) = 6.2\).
  1. The price level fell slightly, from \(6.4\) toward \(6.2\), and real GDP moved closer to potential GDP but is not all the way there yet in this snapshot. In practice this adjustment takes longer than the inflationary case because it requires nominal wages to fall, and wages are sticky downward (Chapter 6): workers and firms both resist outright pay cuts, so this leg of the self-correction can take years rather than months.

The Short-Run Phillips Curve

Introducing the Short-Run Phillips Curve

The short-run Phillips curve shows an inverse relationship between the unemployment rate and the inflation rate: when unemployment is low, inflation tends to be high, and when unemployment is high, inflation tends to be low.

  • This is not a new, separate theory. It is the demand-pull side of the AD/AS model from Chapter 12, just relabeled onto different axes.
  • AD/AS axes: real GDP and the price level. Phillips curve axes: unemployment and inflation.
  • A rightward shift in AD (demand-pull pressure) raises real GDP and the price level and lowers unemployment and raises inflation, at the same time. They are two views of the same event.

Low unemployment means a hot labor market, output above potential, and rising prices, all three go together.

Example: Reading a Short-Run Phillips Curve

A short-run Phillips curve for this economy is given by \(\pi = 6 - 0.5u\), where \(\pi\) is the inflation rate (percent) and \(u\) is the unemployment rate (percent).

  1. What inflation rate does this curve predict when unemployment is \(4\) percent?
  2. What inflation rate does this curve predict when unemployment is \(8\) percent?
  3. As unemployment falls, what happens to inflation, and why does that match the demand-pull story from Chapter 12?

Solution: Reading a Short-Run Phillips Curve

  1. \(\pi = 6 - 0.5(4) = 4\) percent.
  1. \(\pi = 6 - 0.5(8) = 2\) percent.
  1. As unemployment falls, predicted inflation rises. In AD/AS terms this is the same as AD shifting right: real GDP moves up toward and past potential (unemployment falls) while the price level rises faster (inflation rises). Same event, two labels.

The Long-Run Phillips Curve

Recap: The Natural Rate of Unemployment (Chapter 9)

Chapter 9 defined the natural rate of unemployment: frictional plus structural unemployment, the unemployment that remains even when the economy is producing at potential GDP.

  • It is not zero. Some frictional unemployment (people between jobs) and structural unemployment (skills mismatch) always exist, even in a healthy economy.
  • Potential GDP, from Chapter 12, is defined as the output level the economy produces when unemployment is at its natural rate.

The natural rate of unemployment is the labor-market twin of potential GDP. That connection is exactly what makes the long-run Phillips curve vertical.

The Long-Run Phillips Curve Is Vertical

The long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment.

  • In the long run, after nominal wages and price expectations fully adjust (the same self-correction mechanism from earlier in this chapter), the economy always settles back at the natural rate of unemployment.
  • This means any inflation rate is compatible with the natural rate of unemployment in the long run: there is no lasting tradeoff to exploit.
  • This is the exact Phillips-curve analogue of LRAS being vertical at potential GDP: in both cases, once wages fully adjust, the price level (or inflation rate) has no lasting effect on real quantities (output, or unemployment).

Low unemployment can be bought with high inflation only temporarily, never permanently.

Example: Short Run or Long Run?

For each scenario, identify whether it describes a point on the short-run Phillips curve or the long-run Phillips curve.

  1. Unemployment is temporarily below the natural rate, and inflation is rising as firms bid up wages to attract scarce workers.
  2. The economy has fully adjusted: unemployment has returned to the natural rate, and this holds true whether current inflation is \(2\) percent or \(6\) percent.

Solution: Short Run or Long Run?

  1. Short run. Unemployment away from the natural rate, moving along a downward-sloping short-run Phillips curve, is exactly the temporary tradeoff described earlier in this section.
  1. Long run. Unemployment sits at the natural rate regardless of the inflation rate, that is precisely what a vertical long-run Phillips curve means: no tradeoff remains once adjustment is complete.

Why the Short-Run Tradeoff Disappears

Expected Inflation Catches Up

Why doesn’t the short-run tradeoff just persist forever? Because expected inflation eventually catches up to actual inflation.

  • Chapter 9 distinguished nominal from real values; Chapter 15 discussed how inflation expectations form and how forward guidance tries to shape them.
  • If actual inflation stays elevated, workers and firms gradually raise their expected inflation to match it, workers demand bigger raises, firms expect to be able to charge more.
  • Once expected inflation catches up to actual inflation, the short-run Phillips curve itself shifts up.
  • Unemployment then drifts back toward the natural rate, even if inflation stays elevated, exactly the labor-market mirror of SRAS shifting left as wages catch up in the AD/AS story.

The tradeoff was only ever temporary: it exists exactly as long as expectations lag reality.

Two Short-Run Curves, One Long-Run Anchor

Both short-run curves pass through the natural rate of unemployment, illustrated here as \(5\) percent (illustrative), just at different inflation rates. As expected inflation rises from \(2\) percent to \(5\) percent, the whole short-run curve shifts up: the economy moves from point A to point B, unemployment ends up back at the same natural rate either way.

Discussion: Tying It Together

  1. If a central bank tries to hold unemployment permanently below the natural rate using expansionary policy, what do you predict happens to expected inflation over time, and what eventually happens to unemployment anyway?
  2. Chapter 15 discussed forward guidance and credible commitments to low inflation. Why might a credible central bank find it easier to avoid this problem than one nobody believes?

A central bank that is not credible has to actually deliver low inflation to convince people. A credible one gets some of that anchoring for free, through expectations alone.

Real Data: Unemployment and Inflation Expectations

  1. Look for a period when this rate fell sharply. Based on this chapter, would you expect inflation to have been rising or falling around the same time?

Answer: rising, a falling unemployment rate moving along the short-run Phillips curve typically comes with rising inflation, the demand-pull side of AD/AS relabeled.

  1. Does this series ever settle at a single constant value for long? What does that suggest about the “natural rate” as a fixed number?

Answer: no, it moves constantly; the natural rate is a useful long-run anchor for the model, not a literal number the economy sits at every month.

  1. Why can’t this graph alone tell you whether unemployment is currently above, below, or at the natural rate?

Answer: the natural rate itself is not directly observed. It has to be estimated separately, from frictional and structural unemployment (Chapter 9), the actual unemployment rate alone doesn’t reveal it.

Real Data: Prices and Inflation Expectations

  1. This series is an index level, not a growth rate. How would you calculate the inflation rate from it?

Answer: the percent change in the index from one period to a year earlier, that percent change is the inflation rate plotted on a Phillips curve’s vertical axis.

  1. Series MICH (University of Michigan: Inflation Expectation) tracks what households expect inflation to be. Why does this chapter care about that series specifically, rather than just actual inflation?

Answer: because it is the gap between actual and expected inflation that drives the short-run Phillips curve’s position; once MICH catches up to actual inflation, the short-run tradeoff disappears.

  1. If MICH rose sharply for several months in a row, what would this chapter predict happens to the short-run Phillips curve?

Answer: it shifts up, the same unemployment rate now comes with higher inflation, exactly the point A to point B shift on the two-curve chart earlier in this section.

Wrapping Up the Macro Policy Story

Why Policy Exists Alongside Self-Correction

If the economy self-corrects on its own, why does this course spend two full chapters on policy?

  • The self-correction mechanism is real, but it can be slow and painful, especially closing a recessionary gap, since nominal wages are sticky downward (Chapter 6) and can take years to fall.
  • Waiting it out means tolerating high unemployment for however long wage adjustment takes.
  • Fiscal policy (Chapter 13, changing government spending and taxes) and monetary policy (Chapter 15, changing interest rates through the Fed) exist precisely to speed up the return to potential GDP, rather than waiting for wages to adjust on their own.

Self-correction is the economy’s default setting. Policy is the choice to intervene rather than wait.

The Whole Policy Story, One Diagram

  • Chapter 12: built AD/AS, defined potential GDP and the inflationary/recessionary gap.
  • Chapter 13: fiscal policy, G and T, shifts AD directly to close a gap faster, subject to recognition, administrative, and operational lags.
  • Chapter 15: monetary policy, the Fed’s toolkit, shifts AD through interest rates and investment, generally faster to act than fiscal policy but weaker at the zero lower bound.
  • Chapter 17 (this chapter): what happens if nobody acts. Wages eventually adjust, SRAS shifts, and the economy returns to potential GDP on its own, just possibly slowly.
  • The Phillips curve: the same self-correction story, relabeled onto unemployment and inflation, and the reason a central bank’s credibility (Chapter 15’s forward guidance) matters for keeping inflation expectations anchored.

Every policy chapter in this course is really answering the same question: how fast, and at what cost, should we get back to potential GDP?

Discussion: Choosing to Wait or Choosing to Act

  1. Suppose the economy is in a mild recessionary gap. What would push a policymaker toward waiting for self-correction rather than using fiscal or monetary policy?
  2. What would push a policymaker toward acting quickly instead?

There is no universally correct answer, this tradeoff, between the pain of waiting and the risks of active policy (timing, credibility, unintended side effects), is exactly what makes macroeconomic policy debates genuinely difficult, not just a matter of picking the “correct” model.

Wrap Up

  • The self-correction mechanism: an inflationary gap pushes wages up and shifts SRAS left until the economy returns to potential GDP at a higher price level; a recessionary gap pushes wages down (slowly, since wages are sticky downward) and shifts SRAS right until the economy returns to potential GDP at a lower price level.
  • The short-run Phillips curve is the demand-pull side of AD/AS relabeled onto unemployment and inflation: low unemployment goes with high inflation, in the short run.
  • The long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment (Chapter 9), the labor-market twin of LRAS being vertical at potential GDP: no lasting tradeoff exists once wages and expectations fully adjust.
  • The short-run tradeoff disappears as expected inflation catches up to actual inflation, shifting the short-run Phillips curve until unemployment drifts back to the natural rate.
  • Fiscal policy (Chapter 13) and monetary policy (Chapter 15) exist because self-correction, while real, can be slow and painful. Policy is a choice about speed, not a replacement for the underlying mechanism.

That closes out the core macro policy story of this course: how the economy reaches equilibrium, how it drifts away, and how it finds its way back, whether on its own or with help. From here, the course turns to an original capstone lecture connecting macroeconomics to the environment.
