Organizing an Economy

Every Economy Must Answer the Same Questions

No matter how a society is organized, it faces the same underlying problem: scarce resources, unlimited wants. Every economic system has to answer:

  1. What will be produced?
  2. How will it be produced?
  3. Who will get the goods and services?
  4. How will the system accommodate change?
  5. How will the system promote progress?

The only thing that differs between economic systems is who answers these questions, and how.

Two Ways to Answer Them

Command Economy

  • Property resources are owned by the government
  • A central planning board decides what gets produced, how, and for whom
  • Little role for individual choice in production decisions

Market System

  • Private individuals and firms own most property resources
  • Decisions are coordinated by markets, through the choices of buyers and sellers acting in their own self-interest
  • Most economies today are a mix, with markets doing most of the coordinating

The system we will focus on in this course is the market system, since it describes the United States and most of the world’s largest economies.

Characteristics of the Market System

What Makes a Market System Work

  • Private property: the right to own, control, and dispose of land and capital
  • Freedom of enterprise and choice: firms are free to produce what they choose, workers are free to take any job they qualify for, consumers are free to spend their income how they wish
  • Self-interest: each person and firm pursues what they believe is best for themselves
  • Competition: independent buyers and sellers, free to enter or exit a market
  • Markets and prices: the mechanism that coordinates millions of independent decisions
  • A limited but active government: enforcing contracts and protecting property rights
  • Use of money: a medium of exchange that replaces inconvenient barter

Why Self-Interest Coordinates Rather Than Wrecks the Economy

Self-interest sounds like it should produce chaos. Instead it gives the system direction.

  • Entrepreneurs try to maximize profit
  • Resource owners want the best price for what they sell
  • Workers choose the job with the best pay and conditions
  • Consumers try to get the most satisfaction for their money

Each person pursuing their own interest is, in effect, forced to serve the interests of others: a business only earns profit by producing something other people are willing to pay for.

Barter, Then Money

Before money, exchange required barter, trading goods directly for other goods.

Barter requires a double coincidence of wants: you need to find someone who has what you want and wants what you have, at the same time.

That is rare and slow. Money is a social invention that solves the problem, a medium of exchange that lets a baker sell bread for money, then use that money to buy anything else, without needing the buyer of bread to happen to sell something the baker wants.

What Will Be Produced?

Profit Is the Signal

In a market system, goods that earn a profit keep getting produced. Goods that lose money stop being produced.

\[ \text{Profit} = \text{Total Revenue} - \text{Total Cost} \]

\[ \text{Total Revenue} = \text{Price} \times \text{Quantity} \]

Consumers signal what they want with dollar votes: every purchase is a vote for more of that good to be produced.

Example: Total Revenue

A small kitchen gadget company sells spatulas for $8 each and sells 90 of them this month.

What is total revenue?

\[ \text{Total Revenue} = \$8 \times 90 = \$720 \]

Now suppose the price rises to $9 and the company still sells 90 spatulas.

\[ \text{Total Revenue} = \$9 \times 90 = \$810 \]

Total revenue rose by $90, purely from the price increase, with no change in quantity sold.

Why “Dollar Votes” Reshape What Gets Produced

When consumer tastes shift, dollar votes shift with them, and firms follow the money.

  • Rising health consciousness pushed a soft drink company known for cola to expand into bottled water and juice
  • A toy that fails with focus groups, or draws a negative reaction before release, can get pulled and redesigned before it ever reaches shelves
  • A product that never finds a market, like a flavored soda that consumers simply did not want, gets discontinued

Firms are free to produce whatever they want, but they only stay in business if what they produce is what consumers are willing to pay for.

How Will It Be Produced?

Firms Choose the Least Cost Method

Competition disciplines how goods get produced, not just what gets produced.

For any good, there is usually more than one possible combination of land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurial ability that can produce it. Firms have a strong incentive to find the combination that minimizes total cost.

Why? Because a firm using a more expensive method than its rivals will have higher costs, and either has to charge a higher price and lose customers, or accept a smaller profit margin. Competition drives high cost producers out of business.

Example: Choosing the Least Cost Method

A firm can produce 100 units of a good using three different techniques. Labor costs $2 per unit, land costs $1 per unit, and capital costs $3 per unit.

Resource Technique 1 Technique 2 Technique 3
Labor (4 units) 4 2 1
Land (1 unit) 1 3 4
Capital (1 unit) 1 1 2

Which technique should the firm choose?

Least Cost Method: Solution

  • Technique 1: (4 x $2) + (1 x $1) + (1 x $3) = $12
  • Technique 2: (2 x $2) + (3 x $1) + (1 x $3) = $10
  • Technique 3: (1 x $2) + (4 x $1) + (2 x $3) = $12

Answer: Technique 2, it produces the same 100 units for the lowest total cost.

A Price Change Shifts the Least Cost Method

A firm can produce 100 units of good X using three methods. Labor costs $2 per unit, land costs $3 per unit, capital costs $5 per unit, and entrepreneurial ability costs $10 per unit (1 unit needed under every method).

Resource Method 1 Method 2 Method 3
Labor 8 13 10
Land 4 3 3
Capital 4 2 4
  1. Which method is the least expensive way to produce the 100 units?
  2. If X sells for 70 cents per unit, what is the firm’s economic profit?
  3. If the price of labor then rises from $2 to $3 per unit, does the least cost method change? If so, to which one?

Least Cost Method with a Price Change: Review

Part a. Total cost of each method (entrepreneurship adds $10 to every method, so it does not affect which is cheapest):

  • Method 1: (8 x $2) + (4 x $3) + (4 x $5) + $10 = $16 + $12 + $20 + $10 = $58
  • Method 2: (13 x $2) + (3 x $3) + (2 x $5) + $10 = $26 + $9 + $10 + $10 = $55
  • Method 3: (10 x $2) + (3 x $3) + (4 x $5) + $10 = $20 + $9 + $20 + $10 = $59

Method 2 is least expensive at $55.

Part b. Revenue = 100 units x $0.70 = $70. Profit = $70 - $55 = $15.

Part c. At $3 labor, Method 1 costs $24 + $12 + $20 + $10 = $66. Method 2 costs $39 + $9 + $10 + $10 = $68. Method 3 costs $30 + $9 + $20 + $10 = $69. Method 1 becomes the least cost choice at $66, so the firm switches production methods, and its economic profit falls from $15 to $70 - $66 = $4.

Who Gets the Output? And Two More Questions

Who Gets the Goods and Services?

In a market system, the answer is whoever is willing and able to pay for them.

  • Income determines purchasing power, and income itself is earned by supplying resources: labor, land, capital, or entrepreneurial ability
  • Prices ration scarce goods among competing buyers, those willing to pay the market price get the good, those who are not, do not
  • This is different from a command economy, where a central authority decides the distribution directly

How Does the System Accommodate Change and Promote Progress?

Accommodating Change

  • The market system is dynamic, not static
  • Consumer tastes shift, and prices and profits communicate that shift to producers
  • Higher prices and profit in a growing category pull resources toward it

Promoting Progress

  • The market system rewards firms that innovate with higher profit
  • Creative destruction: new products and methods continually replace older, inferior ones
  • Economist Joseph Schumpeter described this as the process that “incessantly revolutionizes the economic structure from within”

Creative Destruction in Action

Recorded music has been through several rounds of creative destruction, each new format destroying the market for the one before it.

  • 1948: the vinyl record
  • 1963 to 1964: cassette tapes and 8 track tapes
  • 1982: the compact disc
  • 1992: MP3 players
  • 2002 onward: streaming, accelerated by the smartphone

Each new technology did not just add a new option, it largely destroyed the market for the one that came before.

The Circular Flow Model

Two Economic Units, Two Markets

The circular flow model is a simplified picture of how money and goods move through a market economy. It has two types of economic units, and two types of markets connecting them.

  • Households: own all the factors of production (land, labor, capital, entrepreneurial ability) and buy the goods and services businesses produce
  • Businesses: buy factors of production to produce goods and services, then sell those goods and services
  • Resource market: where households sell resources and businesses buy them
  • Product market: where businesses sell output and households buy it

The Circular Flow, Visualized

Blue arrows are real flows, actual resources and goods and services physically moving. Orange arrows are money flows, payments moving in the opposite direction. Every real flow is matched by a money flow going the other way.

Both Buyers and Sellers, Depending on the Market

Notice that households and businesses each switch roles depending on which market you look at.

  • In the resource market, households are sellers (of labor, land, capital) and businesses are buyers
  • In the product market, businesses are sellers (of goods and services) and households are buyers

Every economic unit in this model is simultaneously a buyer and a seller, just in different markets.

Example: Following the Dollars

Suppose in a simplified economy, households earn $4,000 in wages this month by working for businesses, and spend $3,500 of it buying goods and services from those same businesses. They save the remaining $500.

  • The $4,000 in wages is a money flow through the resource market (businesses pay households for labor)
  • The $3,500 in spending is a money flow through the product market (households pay businesses for goods)
  • The $500 saved does not immediately flow back to a business, it leaves the simple circular flow as a leakage

Discussion: Why the Model Is a Simplification

The basic circular flow leaves out government, the financial system, and the rest of the world entirely.

  1. If a household saves part of its income at a bank instead of spending it, where does that money go, and could it still end up back in the circular flow?
  2. Where would government tax collection and government spending fit into this picture?
  3. Where would a household buying an imported product fit into this picture?

We will add these missing pieces, government, financial markets, and the foreign sector, to a fuller version of this model later in the course.

Real Data: The Market System in the Numbers

Consumer Spending Is the Engine of the Product Market

Series PCE

  1. This series tracks the dollar flow from households to businesses through the product market. Has it ever fallen for a sustained period? What might cause that?

Answer: yes, notably in the 2020 pandemic recession. Sustained drops usually come from recessions, when income falls or uncertainty leads households to cut spending.

  1. If this number rises every month, what does that tell you about the money flow moving the other direction through the resource market?

Answer: rising spending means businesses earn more revenue, which flows back to households as wages, rent, interest, and profit through the resource market.

  1. Which of the five fundamental questions does this data help answer: what, how, who, change, or progress?

Answer: mainly “what will be produced,” since consumer spending is the dollar-vote signal telling businesses what to produce more or less of.

Wrap Up: The Market System

  • The market system answers the five fundamental questions through decentralized decisions, not central planning
  • Private property, self-interest, and competition are what make decentralized decisions add up to a coordinated economy
  • Profit and loss decide what gets produced, competition disciplines how, and income determines who gets it
  • The circular flow model shows how households and businesses connect through the resource market and the product market, with every real flow matched by an opposite money flow
