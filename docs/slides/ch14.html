What Is Money?

Money Without a Common Medium: Barter

Imagine an economy with no money at all, just barter: trading goods and services directly for other goods and services.

  • A barter trade requires a double coincidence of wants: you must find someone who has what you want and wants what you have.
  • Finding that match is slow and costly. A baker who wants shoes has to find a shoe seller who happens to want bread, not shoes from a competitor.

Money exists to eliminate the need for this double coincidence of wants.

The Three Functions of Money

Economists define money by what it does, not by what it’s made of. Anything that performs these three functions is money:

  1. Medium of exchange: money is accepted in trade for goods and services, eliminating the need for a double coincidence of wants.
  2. Unit of account: money is the common measure used to price everything, so values are comparable (a car costs “$20,000,” not “14 cows”).
  3. Store of value: money lets you hold wealth over time, so purchasing power earned today is still usable later.

Medium of Exchange, in Practice

The medium of exchange function is what makes money money in everyday use: sellers accept it because they know others will accept it from them in turn.

  • This is why acceptability matters more than what money is physically made of, covered shortly.
  • Without a widely accepted medium of exchange, every transaction would require a barter-style match.

Unit of Account, in Practice

The unit of account function lets an economy express the value of everything in the same terms.

  • Prices, wages, debts, and contracts are all stated in dollars, making it possible to compare a haircut, a car, and an hour of labor on the same scale.
  • Without a common unit of account, every good would need a separate exchange rate against every other good, an enormous number of prices to track.

Connecting Back: Inflation and the Store-of-Value Function (Chapter 9)

Chapter 9 covered inflation, a sustained rise in the general price level, measured by the CPI.

  • Inflation erodes the store of value function most directly: a dollar saved today buys less in the future if prices keep rising.
  • Severe inflation also damages the other two functions: prices change so often that money is a poor unit of account, and people may start refusing money as a medium of exchange, preferring to barter or use a more stable foreign currency instead.

The worse inflation gets, the less money can actually do its job.

Example: How Fast Does Inflation Erode Savings?

Recall the Rule of 70 from Chapter 8: divide 70 by a percentage growth (or in this case, inflation) rate to estimate the number of years for a quantity to double, or in this case, for savings to lose half their purchasing power.

An economy has an inflation rate of \(5\%\) per year (illustrative).

  1. Using the Rule of 70, roughly how many years until money sitting in savings loses half its purchasing power?
  2. What does this tell you about the store-of-value function of money during periods of high inflation?

Solution: How Fast Does Inflation Erode Savings?

  1. Years to lose half of purchasing power \(= 70 / 5 = 14\) years.
  1. At just \(5\%\) inflation, savings held as cash lose half their real value within about 14 years. At higher inflation rates, this happens much faster (at \(10\%\) inflation, about 7 years). This is exactly why the Fed treats price stability as a core goal: severe or unpredictable inflation makes money a much worse store of value.

The Money Supply: M1 and M2

Measuring the Money Supply: M1

Economists track the money supply using specific, measurable categories. The narrowest and most liquid is M1:

  • Currency in circulation: coins and paper currency held by the public (outside banks).
  • Checkable deposits: balances in checking accounts that can be spent directly, by check or debit card, without any delay.

M1 includes only the most immediately spendable forms of money.

Measuring the Money Supply: M2

M2 is a broader measure: it includes everything in M1, plus several “near-monies”:

  • Savings deposits
  • Money market mutual fund balances
  • Other assets that are close to money, easily converted to cash or checkable deposits, but not quite as instantly spendable as M1.

M2 is less liquid than M1, since near-monies typically take an extra step (a transfer or a sale) before they can be spent directly.

What “Backs” Money Today?

A natural question: what makes a dollar bill actually worth something?

  • Historically, money evolved from commodity money (goods with intrinsic value, like gold or silver coins) to paper money backed by a commodity: early goldsmiths issued paper receipts for gold on deposit, and those receipts began circulating as money themselves.
  • Today’s U.S. dollar is fiat money: it has value because the government declares it legal tender and, critically, because people accept it in exchange for goods and services.

Modern money is backed by acceptability and the government’s guarantee, not by gold or any other commodity.

Real Data: M1 and M2 Money Supply

  1. M2 is always a larger number than M1. Why must that be true, given how each is defined?

Answer: M2 is defined as M1 plus additional near-monies (savings deposits, money market funds), so it can never be smaller than M1.

  1. Both series have grown substantially over time. Does that alone tell you whether inflation is a problem?

Answer: not by itself. Growth in the money supply is only one input into inflation; it has to be compared with growth in real output and how fast money changes hands.

  1. Notice any sharp, unusual jump in the M2 series around 2020. What might explain it?

Answer: a period of unusually large deposit growth, consistent with the extraordinary policy response (fiscal transfers and Fed actions) during the COVID-19 recession.

The Federal Reserve System

The Federal Reserve: The Nation’s Central Bank

The Federal Reserve System (“the Fed”) is the central bank of the United States, created in 1913. It is often described with three words:

  • Central: it sits at the center of the banking system, coordinating monetary policy nationwide.
  • Quasi-public: it blends private ownership (the regional Reserve Banks are technically owned by member commercial banks) with public control (its leaders are appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate, and it operates in the public interest, not for private profit).
  • Bankers’ bank: it does for commercial banks what commercial banks do for the public, it holds their deposits (reserves) and makes loans to them.

The Structure of the Fed

  • The Board of Governors: seven members, based in Washington, D.C., who oversee the entire System and set key policies.
  • 12 regional Federal Reserve Banks: located across the country (for example, in New York, Chicago, and San Francisco), each serving banks in its own district.
  • The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC): the Fed’s key policymaking body, made up of the Board of Governors plus a rotating group of regional Bank presidents. The FOMC meets regularly to set the Fed’s monetary policy stance.

Chapter 15 focuses directly on the FOMC and the tools it uses.

Why Structure This Way?

  • A central board keeps monetary policy coordinated and consistent nationwide, rather than fragmented across independent regional banks.
  • The quasi-public structure is meant to insulate monetary policy decisions from short-term political pressure, while still keeping the Fed accountable to elected officials who appoint its leadership.
  • Serving as bankers’ bank lets the Fed directly influence the banking system: holding bank reserves, clearing payments between banks, and lending to banks when needed.

Fractional Reserve Banking

Fractional Reserve Banking

Banks do not keep 100% of deposits sitting idle. Under fractional reserve banking, a bank holds only a fraction of its deposits as reserves and lends out the rest.

  • Reserves held at the bank (or at the Fed) earn little to nothing; loans earn interest. Banks profit by lending.
  • This is exactly how banks create the ability to lend: a deposit generates loanable funds, which become new spending, which often becomes a new deposit somewhere else in the banking system.

This same feature that makes banking profitable also makes banks vulnerable, covered next.

Bank Runs and Deposit Insurance

Because banks hold only a fraction of deposits in reserve, they cannot repay every depositor at once.

  • A bank run occurs when many depositors, fearing a bank might fail, all try to withdraw their money at the same time, which can actually cause the failure they feared.
  • The policy response: deposit insurance, provided in the U.S. by the FDIC (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation), which guarantees deposits up to a set limit.

Deposit insurance removes the incentive to run: if your deposit is guaranteed regardless of what other depositors do, there is no reason to rush to withdraw first.

How New Loans Expand the Money Supply

When a bank makes a new loan, it typically does not hand the borrower a bag of existing cash, it creates a new checkable deposit for the borrower. That new deposit is spendable money the instant it’s created.

  • When the borrower spends it, the money often ends up as a new deposit somewhere else in the banking system.
  • Since checkable deposits are part of M1, this process expands the money supply.
  • The reverse also holds: when an existing loan is paid down, checkable deposits shrink, and the money supply contracts.

Lending expands the money supply. Repayment contracts it.

Example: Loans and the Money Supply

A bank currently holds $50,000 in excess reserves (reserves beyond what it is required to hold) and decides to lend out the full amount to a small business.

  1. What happens to the bank’s outstanding loans?
  2. When the business spends the loan and it is deposited elsewhere in the banking system, what happens to total checkable deposits?
  3. What happens to the money supply (M1)?
  4. Suppose instead the business later repays the full $50,000 loan. What happens to checkable deposits and the money supply?

Solution: Loans and the Money Supply

  1. The bank’s outstanding loans rise by $50,000.
  1. Total checkable deposits rise by $50,000, since the loan proceeds become a new deposit once spent.
  1. Because checkable deposits are part of M1, the money supply rises by $50,000.
  1. Repayment reverses the process: checkable deposits fall by $50,000, and the money supply contracts by $50,000. Lending expands the money supply; repaying loans shrinks it.

The Demand for Money

Why Hold Money? Two Motives

The demand for money asks: how much of their wealth do people want to hold in the form of money (rather than in interest-bearing assets)? There are two components:

  1. Transactions demand: money held to make everyday purchases. This depends on the level of nominal GDP, more spending in the economy means more money is needed on hand for transactions, regardless of the interest rate.
  2. Asset demand: money held as a store of value, as an alternative to bonds or other interest-bearing assets. This depends inversely on the interest rate.

Asset Demand and the Interest Rate

Why does asset demand fall as the interest rate rises?

  • Holding money (cash, a checking balance) earns little or no interest.
  • The interest rate is the opportunity cost of holding money instead of an interest-bearing asset like a bond.
  • When interest rates are high, that opportunity cost is high, so people want to hold less money and more interest-bearing assets. When interest rates are low, the opportunity cost is small, so people are willing to hold more money.

Total money demand is the horizontal sum of transactions demand (fixed) and asset demand (downward-sloping).

Example: Total Money Demand

In an economy, transactions demand is fixed at $400 billion (illustrative), regardless of the interest rate. Asset demand depends on the interest rate as follows:

Interest rate Asset demand ($ billions)
2% 300
4% 250
6% 200
8% 150
10% 100
  1. Compute total money demand at each interest rate.
  2. As the interest rate rises, what happens to total money demand? Why?

Solution: Total Money Demand

  1. Total demand \(=\) transactions demand \(+\) asset demand:
Interest rate Transactions Asset Total demand
2% 400 300 700
4% 400 250 650
6% 400 200 600
8% 400 150 550
10% 400 100 500
  1. Total money demand falls as the interest rate rises, entirely because asset demand falls, transactions demand doesn’t change with the interest rate at all.

Money Market Equilibrium

The Money Market

The money market determines the equilibrium interest rate, just as an ordinary market determines an equilibrium price.

  • The “price” in this market is the interest rate.
  • The “quantity” is the amount of money people hold.
  • Money demand slopes downward (as just shown).
  • Money supply is set by the Fed and, for a given policy stance, does not depend on the interest rate, so it is drawn as a vertical line.
  • Equilibrium occurs where the money demand curve crosses the fixed money supply line.

Example: Finding the Equilibrium Interest Rate

Using the money demand schedule from the previous example (transactions demand \(= 400\) billion, plus the asset demand table), suppose the Fed sets the money supply at \(Ms = 600\) billion (illustrative).

  1. At which interest rate does total money demand equal \(600\) billion?
  2. What is the equilibrium interest rate?
  3. What would happen to the interest rate if the money supply were higher than \(600\) billion, say \(700\) billion, with demand unchanged?

Solution: Finding the Equilibrium Interest Rate

  1. From the demand table, total money demand equals \(600\) billion exactly at \(i = 6\%\).
  1. Equilibrium interest rate \(= 6\%\).
  1. If \(Ms\) rose to \(700\) billion, the (now larger) supply would intersect the same downward-sloping demand curve at \(i = 2\%\). A larger money supply, at a given demand, pushes the equilibrium interest rate down.

Harder Problem: A Shift in Money Demand

Suppose nominal GDP rises, so transactions demand increases from \(400\) billion to \(500\) billion. Asset demand at each interest rate is unchanged (same table as before). The money supply stays fixed at \(Ms = 600\) billion.

  1. Compute the new total money demand at each interest rate (2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, 10%).
  2. At which interest rate does the new total demand equal \(600\) billion?
  3. Did the equilibrium interest rate rise or fall compared to before? Explain why, in terms of the shift in the demand curve.

Work through this with a partner or in a small group before checking the next slide.

Review: A Shift in Money Demand

  1. New total demand \(=\) 500 (transactions) \(+\) asset demand:
Interest rate Total demand
2% 800
4% 750
6% 700
8% 650
10% 600
  1. New total demand equals \(600\) billion at \(i = 10\%\).
  1. The equilibrium interest rate rose, from \(6\%\) to \(10\%\). Higher nominal GDP shifted the entire money demand curve to the right (people want to hold more money at every interest rate for transactions), and with the money supply unchanged, a rightward shift in demand raises the equilibrium interest rate.

Connecting Back: The Money Market vs. the Loanable Funds Market (Chapter 6)

Chapter 6 introduced the loanable funds market, where savers and borrowers meet and the real interest rate is set by the flow of saving and investment over time.

The money market covered here is a related, but different, model: it explains the interest rate through the stock of money people want to hold right now (money demand) versus the money supply the Fed has set, not through saving and investment flows.

Both models help explain interest rates, from different angles, and both are used throughout macroeconomics.

Interest Rates and Bond Prices

Bonds: A Quick Refresher

A bond is essentially an IOU: the issuer promises to pay the bondholder a fixed stream of future payments.

  • Once a bond is issued, its promised dollar payments do not change.
  • But bonds are traded on secondary markets after issuance, at whatever price a buyer and seller agree to.
  • Because the payment is fixed but the price can change, the effective interest rate (called the yield) an investor earns depends on what price they paid.

Why Bond Prices and Interest Rates Move Oppositely

Consider a simple perpetuity, a bond that pays a fixed dollar amount every year, forever. Its yield is:

\[ \text{yield} = \frac{\text{annual payment}}{\text{price paid}} \]

  • If the price you pay is higher, that fixed payment represents a smaller percentage return, so the yield is lower.
  • If the price is lower, the same fixed payment represents a larger percentage return, so the yield is higher.

This is why bond prices and interest rates (yields) always move in opposite directions.

Example: Bond Prices and Yields

A perpetuity-style bond pays a fixed $50 per year (illustrative), forever.

  1. If an investor buys the bond for $1,000, what yield do they earn?
  2. If the bond’s price falls to $800, what yield does a new buyer earn?
  3. If the bond’s price rises to $1,250, what yield does a new buyer earn?
  4. Summarize the pattern: as price rises, what happens to yield?

Solution: Bond Prices and Yields

  1. Yield \(= 50 / 1{,}000 = 5\%\).
  1. Yield \(= 50 / 800 = 6.25\%\).
  1. Yield \(= 50 / 1{,}250 = 4\%\).
  1. As the bond price rises (\(800 \to 1{,}000 \to 1{,}250\)), the yield falls (\(6.25\% \to 5\% \to 4\%\)). Higher price, lower effective interest rate, and vice versa.

Why This Matters: A Preview of Chapter 15

This inverse relationship is exactly why the Fed can move interest rates by buying or selling bonds:

  • If the Fed buys bonds, it bids up bond prices, which lowers yields, i.e. lowers interest rates.
  • If the Fed sells bonds, it pushes bond prices down, which raises yields, i.e. raises interest rates.

Chapter 15 calls this tool open market operations, and it’s one of the main ways the Fed actively targets interest rates.
