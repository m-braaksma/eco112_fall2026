Recap: The Money Market and the Fed’s Toolkit

Recap: The Money Market (Chapter 14)

Chapter 14 built the money market: the demand for money slopes down (people hold less money at a higher interest rate, since holding money means giving up interest), and the money supply is set by the Fed. The two together determine the market interest rate.

This chapter is not about deriving that equilibrium again. It is about what the Fed actually does to move it, and what happens next.

Connecting Back: The FOMC (Chapter 14)

Chapter 14 introduced the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), the group inside the Fed that meets regularly to set monetary policy.

  • The FOMC does not set a single interest rate directly. It sets a target range for one specific, extremely important rate: the federal funds rate.
  • Everything in this chapter is about the tools the FOMC uses to hit that target, and what happens to the rest of the economy once it does.

Chapter 14 was about what money and the Fed are. This chapter is about the Fed in action.

The Fed’s Modern Policy Toolkit

Tool 1: The Federal Funds Rate Target

The federal funds rate is the interest rate banks charge each other for overnight loans of reserves.

  • Banks occasionally end the day with more reserves than required, or fewer. They lend to and borrow from each other overnight to cover shortfalls.
  • The FOMC announces a target range for this rate (for example, “4.25 to 4.50 percent”) at each meeting.
  • This is the headline number reported after every FOMC meeting, and it anchors most other short-term interest rates in the economy.

The rest of this section is about how the Fed actually gets the market rate to land inside that target range.

Tool 2: Open Market Operations

Open market operations (OMO) are the Fed’s primary day-to-day tool: buying and selling government bonds in the open market to influence the quantity of bank reserves.

  • The Fed buys government bonds: this pays sellers with new reserves, increasing the supply of reserves banks have available to lend to each other, pushing the federal funds rate down.
  • The Fed sells government bonds: this pulls reserves out of the banking system, pushing the federal funds rate up.

Open market operations are how the Fed nudges the actual market rate toward its announced target.

Recap: Bond Prices and Interest Rates (Chapter 14)

Chapter 14 established the inverse relationship between bond prices and interest rates, and it is exactly what makes open market operations work.

  • When the Fed buys bonds, it increases demand for them, so bond prices rise, and the interest rate on those bonds falls.
  • When the Fed sells bonds, it increases supply in the market, so bond prices fall, and the interest rate rises.

Same relationship from Chapter 14, now put to work as a policy tool rather than just a fact about bonds.

Example: Which Way Should the Fed Trade?

The FOMC wants to lower the federal funds rate target from 4.5 percent to 4.0 percent to support a weakening economy.

  1. Should the Fed buy or sell government bonds to accomplish this?
  2. What happens to bond prices as a result?
  3. What happens to bank reserves as a result?

Solution: Which Way Should the Fed Trade?

  1. The Fed should buy government bonds.
  1. Buying bonds increases demand for them, so bond prices rise.
  1. Paying for those bonds injects new reserves into the banking system, increasing the supply of reserves available for banks to lend overnight, which pushes the federal funds rate down toward the new, lower target.

Tool 3: Interest on Reserve Balances

Interest on reserve balances (IORB) is an administered rate: the interest rate the Fed itself pays banks on the reserves they hold at the Fed.

  • If market rates started drifting below IORB, banks would rather just leave reserves parked at the Fed earning IORB than lend them out cheaply overnight, which pulls the market rate back up.
  • IORB effectively acts as a floor that helps keep the federal funds rate inside the FOMC’s target range, without the Fed needing to trade bonds every single day.

Unlike open market operations, this rate is simply set by the Fed, not moved by buying or selling anything.

Tool 4: The Discount Rate

The discount rate is the interest rate the Fed charges banks for direct, short-term loans from the Fed itself, through what is called the discount window.

  • This is a backup source of funds for a bank that cannot find an overnight loan from another bank at a reasonable rate.
  • The discount rate is normally set above the federal funds rate target, so borrowing directly from the Fed is a last resort, not a first choice.

Together, these four tools, the funds rate target, open market operations, IORB, and the discount rate, are how the Fed manages short-term interest rates day to day.

Real Data: The Federal Funds Rate

  1. Around a recession, which direction would you expect this rate to move, and why?

Answer: down, the Fed typically cuts its target to encourage borrowing and spending during a downturn, using open market operations (bond purchases) to push the effective rate toward the lower target.

  1. Why is this called the effective rate rather than just “the Fed’s rate”?

Answer: the FOMC sets a target range, not a single number; the effective rate is the actual average rate banks pay each other, which the Fed’s tools keep inside that range.

  1. What would a long period where this rate barely moves at all suggest about the Fed’s stance?

Answer: a relatively neutral or “wait and see” stance, the Fed is not actively trying to speed up or slow down borrowing and spending through this tool for now.

Forward Guidance

What Is Forward Guidance?

Forward guidance is the Fed communicating its outlook and its likely future policy path, without changing any rate today.

  • Examples: a statement that rates are “likely to fall over the coming quarters,” or that the Fed expects to “remain patient” before its next move.
  • No bond has been bought or sold yet, no target has moved yet. All that has changed is what the Fed has said about what it plans to do.

If the market believes the Fed, that belief alone can move behavior, before any actual rate change happens.

Why Forward Guidance Works

Forward guidance works because of credibility and expectations.

  • If businesses and investors trust that the Fed will follow through, they do not wait for the actual rate change to act. They adjust spending and investment plans immediately, based on what the Fed says it intends to do.
  • A credible signal of future rate cuts makes borrowing look cheaper soon, so firms may start planning new investment projects now, anticipating better financing conditions ahead.

This is a distinct channel from the tools already covered: no bonds traded, no reserves moved, just expectations doing the work.

Example: Which Is It?

Recall the investment demand curve from Chapter 10: it plots planned investment against the current interest rate, sloping down.

For each event below, is it (a) a movement along the investment demand curve, or (b) a shift of the entire investment demand curve?

  1. The Fed actually cuts the federal funds rate target this week, and firms respond by borrowing more at the new, lower rate.
  2. The Fed issues a statement strongly signaling it expects to cut rates significantly over the next year, and firms increase planned investment at today’s interest rate, anticipating cheaper financing ahead.

Solution: Which Is It?

  1. A movement along the investment demand curve. The interest rate itself actually changed, so investment responds by sliding along the existing curve to a new point.
  1. A shift of the investment demand curve. Today’s interest rate has not changed at all, only expectations about the future have. More investment happens at every current interest rate, so the whole curve shifts right.

The distinction is whether an actual rate change happened (movement along) or only a credible signal about the future did (a shift).

Quantitative Easing and Quantitative Tightening

The Zero Lower Bound Problem

Ordinary open market operations mainly target short-term rates, like the federal funds rate. But what happens when short-term rates are already pushed down close to zero, the so-called zero lower bound, and the economy still needs more support?

  • The Fed cannot cut the federal funds rate much further below zero.
  • Businesses and households also borrow at longer-term rates (a 10-year mortgage, a 20-year corporate bond), which ordinary short-term operations only influence indirectly.

This is the situation that quantitative easing was designed to address.

Quantitative Easing (QE)

Quantitative easing (QE) is the Fed buying longer-term government bonds (and sometimes other securities) in large quantities, directly targeting long-term interest rates rather than the short-term federal funds rate.

  • Buying long-term bonds pushes their prices up and their yields (interest rates) down, the same bond price/interest rate logic from Chapter 14, just applied further out on the maturity spectrum.
  • The goal is extra stimulus: cheaper long-term borrowing for mortgages, business expansion, and other long-horizon spending, used when the usual short-term tool has little room left to work.

Quantitative Tightening (QT)

Quantitative tightening (QT) is the reverse: the Fed lets its long-term bond holdings run off as they mature, or actively sells them.

  • Reducing the Fed’s demand for long-term bonds tends to push their prices down and their yields up.
  • QT is used to withdraw some of the earlier stimulus once it is no longer needed, tightening financial conditions.

QE pushes long-term rates down for extra stimulus. QT lets long-term rates drift back up.

QE/QT vs. Ordinary Open Market Operations

Ordinary open market operations QE / QT
Target Short-term rates (federal funds rate) Long-term rates directly
Typical use Routine, every meeting Only near the zero lower bound, or unwinding it
Securities Mostly short-term Treasury securities Longer-term bonds, in large volume

Same underlying mechanism, buying and selling bonds to move their price and yield, aimed at a different part of the interest rate spectrum.

Real Data: The 10-Year Treasury Rate

  1. Does this long-term rate always move in lockstep with the federal funds rate?

Answer: not exactly, long-term rates also reflect expectations about future inflation and growth, which is part of why the Fed sometimes uses QE and QT to influence this rate more directly.

  1. If the Fed announces a large QE program, what would you expect to happen to this series?

Answer: it would tend to fall, since the Fed’s large-scale bond buying pushes long-term bond prices up and yields down.

  1. Why might a homebuyer with a 30-year mortgage care more about this series than about the federal funds rate itself?

Answer: long-term borrowing costs like mortgage rates track long-term yields such as this one more closely than they track the short-term federal funds rate.

From Interest Rates to Real GDP: The Transmission Mechanism

Connecting Back: Investment Demand and the Multiplier (Chapters 10-11)

Two tools from earlier chapters are exactly what turns a Fed interest rate move into a change in real GDP:

  • The investment demand curve (Chapter 10): a lower interest rate makes more investment projects profitable, so planned investment rises.
  • The multiplier (Chapters 10-11): an initial change in spending gets re-spent through the economy, producing a larger final change in equilibrium GDP. Multiplier \(= 1/\text{MPS} = 1/(1 - \text{MPC})\).

A rate change does not just affect investment once. It ripples through the whole economy via the multiplier.

Connecting Back: AD/AS (Chapter 12)

Chapter 12 built the full AD/AS model: aggregate demand slopes down, short-run aggregate supply slopes up, and their intersection sets both equilibrium real GDP and the price level.

  • A rise in investment spending, amplified by the multiplier, shifts the entire aggregate demand curve, not just one point on it.
  • Where AD settles against AS determines both how much real GDP changes and how much the price level changes.

This is the last link in the chain: interest rates change investment, investment (times the multiplier) shifts AD, and AD/AS determines the final effect on GDP and prices.

The Full Chain: Expansionary Monetary Policy

  1. The Fed lowers its federal funds rate target (using open market operations, and possibly forward guidance or QE).
  2. Lower interest rates raise planned investment spending, a movement along the investment demand curve (Chapter 10).
  3. That initial rise in spending is re-spent through the economy, multiplied (Chapters 10-11).
  4. The larger total increase in spending shifts aggregate demand to the right (Chapter 12).
  5. Equilibrium real GDP rises, and the price level rises somewhat too, depending on how close the economy already is to full employment.

Contractionary monetary policy runs this exact same chain in reverse.

Example: Tracing an Expansionary Policy

The Fed cuts its federal funds rate target. As a result, businesses increase planned investment spending by $25 billion (illustrative). The economy’s MPC is \(0.75\).

  1. Compute the spending multiplier.
  2. Using the multiplier, find the total shift in aggregate demand, measured in additional real GDP.
  3. Which chapter’s model determines how much of that shift shows up as higher real GDP versus a higher price level?

Solution: Tracing an Expansionary Policy

  1. Multiplier \(= 1/(1 - 0.75) = 1/0.25 = 4\).
  1. AD shift \(= 25 \times 4 = 100\). Aggregate demand shifts right by an amount equal to $100 billion in real GDP terms.
  1. Chapter 12’s AD/AS model: along the upward-sloping short-run AS curve, part of this shift raises real GDP and part raises the price level, exactly how much of each depends on how steep AS is at that point.

Harder Problem: Tracing a Contractionary Policy

The economy is overheating, so the Fed raises its federal funds rate target. As a result, businesses cut planned investment spending by $15 billion (illustrative). The economy’s MPC is \(0.8\).

  1. Compute the spending multiplier for this economy.
  2. Using the multiplier, find the total shift in aggregate demand, measured in real GDP.
  3. Which direction does aggregate demand shift, and what happens to equilibrium real GDP and the price level?

Work through this with a partner or in a small group before checking the next slide.

Review: Tracing a Contractionary Policy

  1. Multiplier \(= 1/(1 - 0.8) = 1/0.2 = 5\).
  1. AD shift \(= 15 \times 5 = 75\). Aggregate demand shifts by an amount equal to $75 billion in real GDP terms.
  1. Aggregate demand shifts left (investment fell). Equilibrium real GDP falls, and upward pressure on the price level eases, exactly what a contractionary policy is meant to do when the economy is overheating.

Why Target 2 Percent Inflation, Not Zero?

Connecting Back: Nominal vs. Real Income (Chapter 9)

Chapter 9 distinguished nominal income (dollar amount, unadjusted) from real income (adjusted for inflation, actual purchasing power).

  • A worker’s real wage can fall even if their nominal wage stays exactly the same, as long as prices rise around them.
  • This distinction turns out to matter a great deal for how the Fed thinks about its inflation target.

Greasing the Wheels of the Labor Market

The Fed targets inflation of roughly 2 percent per year, not zero, in part because of how labor markets actually work.

  • Cutting a worker’s nominal wage is extremely difficult in practice: it feels unfair, hurts morale, and workers strongly resist it, even when economic conditions call for a real wage cut.
  • With a small, steady, positive inflation rate, a firm can simply hold nominal wages flat and let inflation quietly erode the real wage over time, achieving the needed real adjustment without ever cutting anyone’s paycheck number.
  • Economists call this “greasing the wheels” of the labor market: a little inflation lets real wages adjust downward gradually, when zero inflation would require nominal wage cuts that rarely happen smoothly.

A target of exactly zero would remove this flexibility entirely.

Discussion: Zero Inflation vs. Low Positive Inflation

  1. Why might a firm find it easier to simply not give a raise this year than to announce an outright pay cut, even if the economic effect on the worker’s real income is similar?
  2. If the Fed instead targeted zero percent inflation, what would you predict happens to unemployment during a downturn, given how sticky nominal wages tend to be?

A small, stable, positive inflation rate is a deliberate policy choice, not an accident or a failure to hit “the real target” of zero.

The Limits of Monetary Policy

Cyclical Asymmetry

Monetary policy is not equally reliable in both directions, economists call this cyclical asymmetry.

  • Slowing an overheating economy tends to work well: raising interest rates reliably discourages borrowing and spending, cooling demand-pull inflation.
  • Reviving a severely depressed economy is much less reliable: even when the Fed pushes interest rates very low, businesses may be too uncertain about the future to borrow, and banks may be too cautious to lend, regardless of how cheap credit has become.

The brake works better than the accelerator.

The Liquidity Trap

A liquidity trap is a situation where interest rates are already very low, but further cuts fail to stimulate more borrowing and lending.

  • Businesses hold back on investment even at rock-bottom rates, because they are worried about weak future demand, not about the cost of borrowing.
  • Banks hold back on lending even with ample reserves, because they are worried about the safety of the loans, not about their own funding costs.
  • In this situation, additional rate cuts (or even QE) have little further traction, the tool has essentially run out of room to work.

This is precisely the situation quantitative easing was designed for, but even QE has limits once expectations and confidence are the real constraint.

Connecting Back: Monetary vs. Fiscal Policy (Chapter 13)

Chapter 13 covered fiscal policy: Congress and the President changing government spending (G) and taxes (T) to shift AD directly.

  • Monetary policy works through interest rates and the Fed, an independent body that can act relatively quickly, without needing new legislation.
  • Fiscal policy works through G and T and Congress, subject to the recognition, administrative, and operational lags covered in Chapter 13.
  • In a liquidity trap, monetary policy loses much of its traction, exactly the situation where fiscal policy, despite its slower legislative process, may be needed to shift AD directly through government spending or tax changes.

Two different levers, and each one tends to be strongest exactly where the other is weakest.

Wrap Up

  • The Fed’s modern toolkit: the federal funds rate target, open market operations (buying/selling bonds moves reserves and short-term rates), interest on reserve balances, and the discount rate.
  • Forward guidance shifts expectations, and can shift the investment demand curve, without any actual rate change; that is what distinguishes it from a movement along the curve.
  • Quantitative easing targets long-term rates directly when short-term rates are near the zero lower bound; quantitative tightening reverses it.
  • The transmission chain: a Fed rate change moves investment (Ch. 10), gets multiplied (Ch. 10-11), shifts AD (Ch. 12), and changes both real GDP and the price level.
  • The Fed targets roughly 2 percent inflation, not zero, partly to let real wages adjust downward gradually even when nominal wages are sticky (Ch. 9).
  • Monetary policy is more reliable at slowing an overheating economy than at pulling one out of a severe recession; a liquidity trap is one reason fiscal policy (Ch. 13) sometimes has to help.
