Discretionary Fiscal Policy and the Multiplier

Connecting Back: The AD/AS Model (Chapter 12)

Chapter 12 built the aggregate demand and aggregate supply (AD/AS) model: aggregate demand slopes down, short-run aggregate supply slopes up, and equilibrium output and the price level are set where they cross.

  • Aggregate demand (AD) can shift right or left, moving equilibrium GDP and the price level.
  • This chapter treats AD/AS as an established tool and asks a policy question: can the government deliberately shift AD on purpose?

Fiscal policy is exactly that: using government spending and taxes as levers to shift AD.

Connecting Back: The Multiplier (Chapters 10-11)

Chapters 10 and 11 showed that a change in spending gets re-spent through the economy, so it produces a larger final change in equilibrium GDP.

  • Spending multiplier: \(1/\text{MPS}\)
  • A change in government purchases (\(\Delta G\)) shifts equilibrium GDP by \(\Delta G \times \text{multiplier}\).
  • A change in taxes (\(\Delta T\)) shifts equilibrium GDP by \(\Delta T \times \text{MPC} \times \text{multiplier}\), smaller than an equal-sized change in G, since part of a tax change is absorbed by saving rather than spending.

This chapter puts these tools to work as deliberate policy.

Connecting Back: Expenditure Gaps and the Business Cycle (Chapters 9-11)

Chapter 9 described the business cycle and the difference between demand-pull inflation (too much spending chasing output) and cost-push inflation (rising input costs). Chapter 11 introduced recessionary and inflationary expenditure gaps.

  • A recessionary gap: equilibrium GDP sits below full-employment (potential) GDP.
  • An inflationary gap: spending is pulling the economy above potential GDP, risking demand-pull inflation.

Fiscal policy is the deliberate attempt to close these gaps by shifting AD.

What Is Discretionary Fiscal Policy?

Discretionary fiscal policy is the deliberate, legislated use of government spending and taxes to stabilize the economy. It requires new legislation, Congress and the President must actively decide to change G or T.

  • This is distinct from automatic stabilizers (covered next section), which work without any new law.
  • In the U.S., policy recommendations often come from bodies like the Council of Economic Advisers, which advises the President on macroeconomic conditions.

Discretionary fiscal policy is a choice. Automatic stabilizers are not.

Who Makes Fiscal Policy?

Fiscal policy in the U.S. is set through the political process, not by a single independent agency.

  • Congress must pass spending bills and tax legislation.
  • The President proposes budgets, signs or vetoes legislation, and receives advice from the Council of Economic Advisers.
  • Because it requires legislation, discretionary fiscal policy can be slow to enact compared to other policy tools, an idea we return to shortly.

Expansionary Fiscal Policy

Expansionary fiscal policy is used to close a recessionary gap, that is, to fight a recession.

  • Increase government spending (G), or
  • Decrease taxes (T), or
  • Some combination of both

Each of these shifts AD to the right, raising equilibrium GDP (and, in AD/AS terms, moving output toward potential GDP).

Contractionary Fiscal Policy

Contractionary fiscal policy is used to close an inflationary gap, that is, to fight demand-pull inflation.

  • Decrease government spending (G), or
  • Increase taxes (T), or
  • Some combination of both

Each of these shifts AD to the left, lowering equilibrium GDP (and easing upward pressure on the price level).

The G-Multiplier vs. the T-Multiplier

\[ \Delta \text{GDP} = \Delta G \times \text{multiplier}, \qquad \text{multiplier} = \frac{1}{\text{MPS}} \]

\[ \Delta \text{GDP} = \Delta T \times \text{MPC} \times \text{multiplier} \]

A dollar of spending has a bigger effect on equilibrium GDP than a dollar of tax change, because a tax change is only partly spent (some is saved), while a spending change is spent in full, immediately.

Example: Closing a Recessionary Gap

An economy has a recessionary gap: equilibrium GDP needs to rise by $300 billion (illustrative) to reach full-employment GDP. The MPC is \(0.75\).

  1. Compute the spending multiplier and the tax multiplier.
  2. If policymakers use only a change in G, how large a change is needed?
  3. If policymakers use only a change in T, how large a change is needed?
  4. Find one combination of a simultaneous change in G and T that closes the gap.

Solution: Closing a Recessionary Gap

  1. Multiplier \(= 1/\text{MPS} = 1/0.25 = 4\). Tax multiplier \(= \text{MPC} \times \text{multiplier} = 0.75 \times 4 = 3\).
  1. G alone: \(\Delta G = 300 / 4 = 75\). Increase G by $75 billion.
  1. T alone: \(\Delta T = 300 / 3 = 100\). Decrease T by $100 billion.
  1. Combination: increase G by $45 billion and decrease T by $40 billion. Check: \(45 \times 4 = 180\), plus \(40 \times 3 = 120\), total \(= 300\). Exactly closes the gap.

Harder Problem: Closing an Inflationary Gap

A different economy has an inflationary gap: equilibrium GDP needs to fall by $240 billion to reach full-employment GDP. The MPC here is \(0.6\).

  1. Compute the spending multiplier and the tax multiplier for this economy.
  2. If policymakers use only a change in G, how large a change is needed, and in which direction?
  3. If policymakers use only a change in T, how large a change is needed, and in which direction?
  4. Find one combination of a simultaneous change in G and T that closes the gap.

Work through this with a partner or in a small group before checking the next slide.

Review: Closing an Inflationary Gap

  1. Multiplier \(= 1/0.4 = 2.5\). Tax multiplier \(= 0.6 \times 2.5 = 1.5\).
  1. G alone: \(\Delta G = 240 / 2.5 = 96\). Decrease G by $96 billion.
  1. T alone: \(\Delta T = 240 / 1.5 = 160\). Increase T by $160 billion.
  1. Combination: decrease G by $60 billion and increase T by $60 billion. Check: \(60 \times 2.5 = 150\), plus \(60 \times 1.5 = 90\), total \(= 240\). Exactly closes the gap.

The Balanced-Budget Multiplier

What if G and T rise by the same dollar amount, so the budget balance doesn’t change? You might guess the effect on GDP is zero. It is not.

  • The spending multiplier (\(1/\text{MPS}\)) is always larger than the tax multiplier (\(\text{MPC}/\text{MPS}\)), because all of a spending increase is spent, while only part of after-tax income is spent.
  • The balanced-budget multiplier shows that an equal increase in G and T still raises equilibrium GDP, by exactly the amount of the increase.

\[ \text{multiplier} - \text{MPC} \times \text{multiplier} = (1 - \text{MPC}) \times \frac{1}{\text{MPS}} = \frac{\text{MPS}}{\text{MPS}} = 1 \]

Example: The Balanced-Budget Multiplier

An economy has MPC \(= 0.75\) (multiplier \(= 4\), tax multiplier \(= 3\), as before, but use fresh numbers here). Congress raises both G and T by $60 billion each, keeping the budget balance unchanged.

  1. What is the GDP effect of the $60 billion increase in G alone?
  2. What is the GDP effect of the $60 billion increase in T alone?
  3. What is the net effect on equilibrium GDP?

Solution: The Balanced-Budget Multiplier

  1. G effect: \(60 \times 4 = 240\) (GDP rises by 240).
  1. T effect: \(60 \times 3 = 180\) (GDP falls by 180).
  1. Net effect: \(240 - 180 = 60\). Equilibrium GDP rises by exactly $60 billion, the same as the dollar increase in G and T. This confirms the balanced-budget multiplier equals 1.

Lags in Fiscal Policy

Discretionary fiscal policy sounds precise on paper, but in practice it is slowed by several lags:

  • Recognition lag: time to realize a recession or inflationary episode is actually happening.
  • Administrative lag: time for Congress to debate, negotiate, and pass legislation.
  • Operational lag: time for approved spending or tax changes to actually affect the economy.

By the time a discretionary fiscal policy takes effect, the economy may already be changing on its own. This is one reason automatic stabilizers, covered next, are valuable.

Automatic Stabilizers

Automatic (Non-Discretionary) Stabilizers

Automatic stabilizers are features built into the tax and transfer system that stabilize the economy without any new legislation.

  • As GDP rises (an expansion), tax revenues automatically rise (with a progressive tax system) and transfer payments (like unemployment benefits) automatically fall. This automatically restrains an overheating expansion.
  • As GDP falls (a recession), tax revenues automatically fall and transfer payments automatically rise. This automatically cushions the downturn.

No one has to pass a law. The system responds on its own, immediately.

Three Tax Structures: Progressive, Proportional, Regressive

The strength of automatic stabilization depends on the shape of the tax system:

  • Progressive tax: the average tax rate rises as income rises.
  • Proportional tax: the average tax rate stays constant as income rises (a flat tax).
  • Regressive tax: the average tax rate falls as income rises.

A more progressive tax system provides more automatic stabilization, since tax revenue (and therefore after-tax income) swings by more automatically as GDP moves.

Example: Progressive vs. Proportional Stabilization

An economy’s GDP falls from $1{,}000 billion to $800 billion (illustrative). Compare two hypothetical tax systems:

  • Proportional system: a flat \(20\%\) average tax rate at every GDP level.
  • Progressive system: average tax rate is \(25\%\) at GDP \(= 1{,}000\), falling to \(15\%\) at GDP \(= 800\).
  1. Compute tax revenue at both GDP levels under each system.
  2. Compute after-tax (disposable) income at both GDP levels under each system.
  3. Which system cushions the fall in disposable income more?

Solution: Progressive vs. Proportional Stabilization

GDP 1,000 GDP 800 Change
Proportional revenue 200 160 -40
Progressive revenue 250 120 -130
Proportional disp. income 800 640 -160
Progressive disp. income 750 680 -70

Tax revenue falls by more under the progressive system ($130 vs. $40), which sounds bad for government revenue, but it means disposable income falls by much less ($70 vs. $160).

The progressive system automatically absorbs more of the GDP shock through taxes, leaving households’ after-tax income, and therefore their spending, more stable.

Automatic Stabilizers in the Real World

  • The progressive federal income tax: as incomes fall in a downturn, households move into lower brackets, so tax bills fall automatically.
  • Unemployment insurance: benefits rise automatically as more people lose jobs, without new legislation.
  • Means-tested transfer programs (food assistance, for example) automatically expand as more households qualify during a downturn.

Automatic stabilizers act instantly, no recognition lag, no administrative lag, no operational lag. Their downside is that they are typically too small, on their own, to fully close a large expenditure gap.

Discussion: Automatic vs. Discretionary Policy

  1. Why might policymakers still want discretionary fiscal policy, even with automatic stabilizers already in place?
  2. During a very deep recession, would you expect automatic stabilizers alone to be enough to close the entire recessionary gap? Why or why not?

Automatic stabilizers cushion the swings. Discretionary fiscal policy is the tool for a deliberate, larger push.

Actual vs. Cyclically Adjusted Budget

The Actual Budget Deficit or Surplus

The actual budget deficit or surplus is simply what literally happened in a given year: actual government spending minus actual government revenue.

  • A budget deficit occurs when spending exceeds revenue in that year.
  • A budget surplus occurs when revenue exceeds spending.

Problem: because of automatic stabilizers, the actual budget can show a large deficit purely because the economy is in a recession, not because policymakers deliberately chose an expansionary stance.

The Cyclically Adjusted Budget

The cyclically adjusted budget (also called the full-employment budget) asks a different question: what would the deficit or surplus be if the economy were operating exactly at full-employment (potential) GDP, using the current tax and spending laws?

  • This strips out the effect of where the economy happens to be in the business cycle.
  • It reveals whether fiscal policy itself is expansionary, neutral, or contractionary, independent of automatic stabilizers.

The actual budget tells you what happened. The cyclically adjusted budget tells you what policymakers actually chose.

Example: Actual vs. Cyclically Adjusted Budget

An economy’s potential GDP is $1{,}000 billion, but actual GDP this year is only $900 billion (a recessionary gap). The tax rate is a flat \(20\%\), and government spending is fixed at $200 billion regardless of GDP.

  1. Compute actual tax revenue and the actual budget balance.
  2. Compute what tax revenue would be if GDP were at its potential level of $1{,}000 billion.
  3. Compute the cyclically adjusted budget balance.
  4. What does the comparison tell you about the stance of fiscal policy?

Solution: Actual vs. Cyclically Adjusted Budget

  1. Actual revenue \(= 0.20 \times 900 = 180\). Actual balance \(= 180 - 200 = -20\) (a $20 billion deficit).
  1. Revenue at potential GDP \(= 0.20 \times 1{,}000 = 200\).
  1. Cyclically adjusted balance \(= 200 - 200 = 0\) (balanced).
  1. The actual $20 billion deficit is entirely a cyclical result of GDP sitting below potential, automatic stabilizers working as intended. The cyclically adjusted budget of \(0\) shows that fiscal policy itself is neither expansionary nor contractionary here, it is neutral.

Public Debt

What Is the Public Debt?

The public debt is the accumulated total of past budget deficits, minus past surpluses. Each year’s deficit adds to the debt; each year’s surplus subtracts from it.

  • The public debt is a stock (accumulated over time), while the deficit or surplus is a flow (measured within one year).
  • A country can run a deficit in some years and a surplus in others; the debt reflects the running total.

Measuring Debt: Absolute Dollars vs. Percent of GDP

The public debt can be measured two ways:

  • Absolute dollar terms: the total dollar amount owed. Useful, but hard to compare across time or across countries of very different sizes.
  • As a percentage of GDP: the debt relative to the size of the economy that has to service it.

The percent-of-GDP measure is more meaningful for comparing debt burdens over time or across countries, since a larger economy can support a larger absolute debt without the burden being any heavier.

Refinancing vs. Retiring the Debt

Governments manage maturing debt in two very different ways:

  • Refinancing (rolling over): selling new bonds to pay off bonds that are maturing. The debt is not paid off, it is simply carried forward.
  • Retiring the debt: actually paying it off, using tax revenue or a budget surplus, so the debt shrinks.

In practice, most government debt is continuously refinanced rather than retired.

Internally Held vs. Externally Held Debt

Public debt can be held by different lenders:

  • Internally held debt: owed to a country’s own citizens, banks, and institutions.
  • Externally held debt: owed to foreign lenders (governments, institutions, or individuals abroad).

Internally held debt mostly redistributes purchasing power within the country, repayment goes from domestic taxpayers to domestic bondholders. Externally held debt is different: repayment sends purchasing power abroad, a real burden that internal debt does not carry in the same way.

Connecting Back: Crowding Out (Chapter 6)

Chapter 6 introduced the market for loanable funds, where borrowers and savers meet and the real interest rate is determined.

  • When the government runs a deficit, it must borrow, competing with private borrowers for the same pool of loanable funds.
  • This added demand for loanable funds can push the real interest rate up.
  • A higher real interest rate discourages some private investment, this is the crowding-out effect.

Crowding out partially offsets the stimulative effect of expansionary fiscal policy: some of the boost from higher G is undone by lower private investment.

Example: Accumulating the Public Debt

A small country starts with zero public debt. Over four years, its budget results (illustrative, in billions) are:

Year Deficit (+) or Surplus (-)
1 Deficit of 50
2 Deficit of 30
3 Surplus of 10
4 Deficit of 20

This country’s GDP in Year 4 is $900 billion.

  1. Compute the accumulated public debt at the end of each year.
  2. Express the Year 4 debt as a percentage of Year 4 GDP.

Solution: Accumulating the Public Debt

  1. End of Year 1: \(0 + 50 = 50\). End of Year 2: \(50 + 30 = 80\). End of Year 3: \(80 - 10 = 70\) (a surplus reduces the debt). End of Year 4: \(70 + 20 = 90\).
  1. Accumulated public debt \(= 90\) billion. Debt-to-GDP \(= 90 / 900 = 10\%\).

A single year’s surplus (Year 3) slowed the growth of the debt, but did not come close to eliminating it. The debt is a running total of every year’s result, not just the most recent one.

Real Data: The Public Debt

  1. Why might the dollar-amount debt series (GFDEBTN) look like it only ever goes up, even in years the economy grew strongly?

Answer: the federal government has run a deficit, adding to the debt, in nearly every recent year, and most maturing debt is refinanced rather than retired, so the total keeps climbing.

  1. Why look at debt as a percent of GDP (GFDEGDQ188S) instead of just the dollar amount?

Answer: percent of GDP accounts for the size of the economy that has to service the debt, making it possible to compare debt burdens meaningfully across different years or countries.

  1. Around a recession, would you expect the percent-of-GDP series to rise for reasons beyond new borrowing?

Answer: yes, GDP itself falls in a recession, which mechanically raises the debt-to-GDP ratio even if the dollar amount of new borrowing were unchanged, on top of automatic stabilizers adding to the deficit.

Looking Ahead: Monetary Policy (Chapters 14-15)

Fiscal policy is only one set of tools for stabilizing the economy. It works through Congress and the President, changing G and T to shift AD, and it is subject to the legislative lags discussed earlier.

  • Chapters 14 and 15 cover monetary policy: the Federal Reserve’s parallel set of tools for shifting AD, working through interest rates and the money supply rather than government spending and taxes.
  • Monetary policy is generally faster to implement than discretionary fiscal policy, since it does not require new legislation.

Two different levers, the same goal: keeping equilibrium GDP close to potential GDP.

Wrap Up

  • Discretionary fiscal policy deliberately changes G and/or T to shift AD: expansionary (raise G, cut T) to close a recessionary gap, contractionary (cut G, raise T) to close an inflationary gap.
  • A dollar of G moves GDP more than a dollar of T, since \(\Delta \text{GDP} = \Delta G \times \text{multiplier}\) versus \(\Delta T \times \text{MPC} \times \text{multiplier}\). The balanced-budget multiplier shows equal increases in G and T still raise GDP, by that same dollar amount.
  • Automatic stabilizers (progressive taxes, transfer payments) cushion the business cycle without new legislation; a more progressive tax system stabilizes more.
  • The cyclically adjusted budget strips out the business cycle to reveal the true stance of fiscal policy, separate from the actual deficit or surplus.
  • The public debt accumulates from past deficits and surpluses, is better measured as a percent of GDP, and can be refinanced or retired, held internally or externally. Deficit-financed borrowing can crowd out private investment.

Looking ahead: Chapters 14 and 15 cover monetary policy, the Federal Reserve’s own set of tools for managing aggregate demand.
