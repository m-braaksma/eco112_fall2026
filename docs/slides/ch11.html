From Sticky Prices to a Spending-Driven Model

Connecting Back: Sticky Prices (Chapter 6)

Chapter 6 introduced sticky prices: in the short run, many prices and especially wages do not adjust quickly when spending changes.

  • If prices were perfectly flexible, a drop in spending would just show up as lower prices, with output and employment barely affected.
  • Because prices are sticky in the short run, a drop in spending instead shows up mostly as lower real output and job losses.

The aggregate expenditures (AE) model in this chapter takes short-run price stickiness as its starting assumption. It asks: if prices are fixed, what determines the level of real GDP?

Connecting Back: The Business Cycle (Chapter 9)

Chapter 9 described that real GDP moves up and down around its long-run trend, the business cycle.

This chapter explains why: total spending in the economy is not automatically the right amount to keep output at its potential level. When spending falls short, GDP falls. When spending overshoots, GDP is pulled above what it can sustain.

The AE model is the mechanism behind the bumps in the business cycle chart.

Say’s Law: The Classical View

Classical economists before the 1930s largely accepted Say’s Law: “supply creates its own demand.”

  • The act of producing goods and services generates exactly enough income to buy everything produced.
  • On this view, the economy is always at, or quickly returns to, full-employment output.
  • Widespread, prolonged unemployment should not be possible.

Keynes’s Challenge

John Maynard Keynes, writing during the Great Depression, challenged Say’s Law directly.

  • Income earned from production does not have to all be spent. Some is saved, and saving does not automatically turn into spending by someone else.
  • If total planned spending falls short of what is needed to buy full-employment output, firms will not produce that much. They cut back.
  • The economy can settle into an equilibrium below full employment, and stay there, with no automatic force pushing it back up.

This is the motivating question for the whole chapter: what determines the economy’s equilibrium level of output, and why might that level fall short of (or exceed) what the economy is capable of producing?

Building the Aggregate Expenditures Schedule

Recap: Tools from Chapter 10

Chapter 10 built two tools this chapter now puts to work:

  • The consumption schedule: how consumption spending rises as real GDP (income) rises, with the slope given by the MPC (marginal propensity to consume).
  • The multiplier: a one-time change in spending gets re-spent through the economy, so a change in spending produces a larger final change in equilibrium GDP. With no government or taxes, multiplier \(= 1/\text{MPS}\).

This chapter uses both tools to build a full model of how equilibrium GDP is determined. We will not re-derive them here, just use them.

A Simple Model, Piece by Piece

We start with the simplest possible case and add realism step by step:

  1. A private, closed economy: just households and businesses, no government, no trade.
  2. Add government purchases and taxes.
  3. Add the rest of the world through net exports.

At each stage, aggregate expenditures (AE) is the total amount that all sectors of the economy plan to spend at each level of real GDP.

The Investment Schedule vs. the Investment Demand Curve

Chapter 10 introduced the investment demand curve: it relates planned investment to the real interest rate. A lower interest rate makes more investment projects profitable, so investment rises.

The investment schedule used in this chapter is different: it relates planned investment to the level of real GDP. In this simple model, we assume planned investment (\(I_g\)) is roughly constant, independent of the current level of GDP, since businesses base investment decisions mainly on interest rates and profit expectations, not on today’s GDP.

Same variable, two different graphs, don’t mix them up.

The AE Schedule: AE = C + Ig

In the private, closed economy, aggregate expenditures is the sum of two components:

\[ AE = C + I_g \]

  • \(C\): consumption spending, which rises with real GDP according to the consumption schedule (MPC).
  • \(I_g\): planned gross private domestic investment, assumed constant at each level of GDP.

Adding a constant \(I_g\) to the consumption schedule shifts it upward by the amount of \(I_g\) at every GDP level, this becomes the AE schedule.

Equilibrium GDP

Where Planned Spending Meets Output

Equilibrium GDP is the level of real GDP at which aggregate expenditures exactly equals real GDP:

\[ AE = \text{Real GDP} \]

At this point, what everyone in the economy plans to spend exactly matches what the economy produces. There is no pressure pushing output up or down.

Graphically, equilibrium is where the AE schedule crosses the 45-degree line, the set of all points where spending equals output.

Graphing Equilibrium

Off Equilibrium: Unplanned Inventories

Away from equilibrium, businesses are surprised by how much they actually sell, and inventories adjust unintentionally.

  • Above equilibrium GDP: AE falls short of GDP. Firms produce more than people plan to buy, so unsold goods pile up as unplanned inventory increases. Firms respond by cutting production, pulling GDP back down.
  • Below equilibrium GDP: AE exceeds GDP. Buyers purchase more than firms planned to produce, so inventories are drawn down, unplanned inventory decreases (negative). Firms respond by raising production, pulling GDP back up.

Unplanned inventory changes are the signal that pulls the economy toward equilibrium.

Solution: Finding Equilibrium and Reading Inventories

  1. Equilibrium is at GDP \(= 500\), since AE $= 500 = $ GDP there.
  1. At GDP \(= 600\): AE \(= 580 < 600\). Spending falls short of output by 20, so unplanned inventories rise. Firms cut production, pulling GDP back down toward 500.
  1. At GDP \(= 450\): AE \(= 460 > 450\). Spending exceeds output by 10, so unplanned inventories fall (become negative). Firms raise production, pulling GDP back up toward 500.

Adding Government to the Model

Government Purchases Shift AE Up

Adding government purchases (\(G\)), assumed constant at every level of GDP, extends the model:

\[ AE = C + I_g + G \]

Adding \(G\) shifts the entire AE schedule upward by the amount of \(G\). Because of the multiplier from Chapter 10, equilibrium GDP rises by more than \(G\) itself, government spending gets re-spent and re-spent through the economy just like any other injection.

Example: Adding Government Purchases

Continuing the same economy (MPC \(= 0.8\), \(I_g = 40\)), suppose the government now adds \(G = 40\) in purchases at every GDP level.

Real GDP C \(I_g\) \(G\) AE
500 460 40 40 ?
600 540 40 40 ?
650 580 40 40 ?
700 620 40 40 ?
750 660 40 40 ?
  1. Fill in the new AE column.
  2. What is the new equilibrium GDP?
  3. Using the multiplier (\(1/\text{MPS}\)), check your answer: by how much should a \(\Delta G = 40\) change equilibrium GDP from the old equilibrium of 500?

Solution: Adding Government Purchases

New equilibrium is at GDP \(= 700\), where AE \(= 700\).

Multiplier check: multiplier \(= 1/0.2 = 5\). \(\Delta G = 40 \times 5 = 200\). Old equilibrium \(500 + 200 = 700\). Matches exactly.

Taxes Reduce Disposable Income

Now add a lump-sum tax (\(T\)). Taxes reduce disposable income (income after taxes), and consumption depends on disposable income, not on GDP directly.

  • A tax increase of \(\Delta T\) reduces consumption at every level of GDP, but not by the full amount of the tax. It falls by \(\text{MPC} \times \Delta T\), since households also reduce saving somewhat, not just spending.
  • This shifts the AE schedule down, by less than the tax itself, and lowers equilibrium GDP.
  • A tax cut works the same way in reverse: it raises consumption by \(\text{MPC} \times \Delta T\) at each GDP level, shifting AE up and raising equilibrium GDP.

Example: Adding a Tax

Continuing the economy above (MPC \(= 0.8\), \(I_g = 40\), \(G = 40\), old equilibrium GDP \(= 700\)), the government now also imposes a lump-sum tax of \(T = 50\).

  1. By how much does consumption fall at each level of GDP?
  2. Write the new AE at GDP \(= 500\): recall \(C = 460\) and AE (with G, before the tax) \(= 540\) at that GDP level.
  3. Where do you expect the new equilibrium GDP to land? Work with a partner or in a small group before checking the next slide.

500 420 40 40 500
550 460 40 40 540

Consumption falls by \(\text{MPC} \times T = 0.8 \times 50 = 40\) at every GDP level.

New equilibrium is back at GDP \(= 500\), since AE \(= 500\) there.

Interesting result: \(G = 40\) alone would have raised equilibrium GDP by 200 (to 700). \(T = 50\) alone would have lowered it by \(\text{MPC} \times T \times \text{multiplier} = 40 \times 5 = 200\). Together, they roughly cancel out, back to the original equilibrium of 500.

The Open Economy and Net Exports

Adding Net Exports

A full, open economy also trades with the rest of the world. Net exports (\(X_n\)) equal exports minus imports:

\[ AE = C + I_g + G + X_n \]

  • A rise in net exports shifts AE up and, through the multiplier, raises equilibrium GDP.
  • A fall in net exports shifts AE down and lowers equilibrium GDP.

\(X_n\) can be positive (net selling to the rest of the world) or negative (net buying).

Example: A Rise in Net Exports

Continuing the economy with \(G = 40\) and \(T = 50\) (equilibrium GDP \(= 500\)), suppose net exports are now \(X_n = +20\).

500 420 40 40 20 ?
550 460 40 40 20 ?
600 500 40 40 20 ?
650 540 40 40 20 ?

  2. What is the new equilibrium GDP?
  3. Check your answer with the multiplier: \(\Delta X_n = 20\), multiplier \(= 5\).

Solution: A Rise in Net Exports

New equilibrium is at GDP \(= 600\), where AE \(= 600\).

Multiplier check: \(\Delta X_n = 20 \times 5 = 100\). Old equilibrium \(500 + 100 = 600\). Matches.

International Linkages

Net exports connect economies to one another. If a major trading partner enters a recession, its demand for our exports falls.

  • Lower foreign demand means lower \(X_n\) for us.
  • A fall in \(X_n\) shifts our AE schedule down and lowers our equilibrium GDP, even though the recession started somewhere else.

This is one reason a downturn in one country’s economy can spread to its trading partners. One brief note: using tariffs to try to boost net exports tends to fail in practice, because trading partners typically retaliate with tariffs of their own, offsetting the intended gain.

Example: A Fall in Net Exports

Suppose instead that a recession abroad causes net exports to fall to \(X_n = -20\) (still with \(G = 40\), \(T = 50\), starting from the original private-economy equilibrium of 500).

  1. By how much does the AE schedule shift compared to the \(X_n = +20\) case?
  2. Using the multiplier, where do you expect the new equilibrium GDP to land?

Work through this with a partner before checking the solution.

Solution: A Fall in Net Exports

Going from \(X_n = +20\) to \(X_n = -20\) is a swing of \(\Delta X_n = -40\).

\(\Delta X_n \times \text{multiplier} = -40 \times 5 = -200\). Starting from the \(X_n = +20\) equilibrium of GDP \(= 600\): \(600 - 200 = 400\).

New equilibrium GDP \(= 400\). A swing in net exports, driven entirely by conditions abroad, moved our equilibrium GDP by 200, illustrating how tightly linked economies are through trade.

Expenditure Gaps

Equilibrium GDP vs. Full-Employment GDP

Equilibrium GDP (where AE crosses the 45-degree line) does not have to equal full-employment GDP (also called potential GDP), the level the economy could produce if all resources were fully employed.

These are two separate reference points on the same graph, and in this fixed-price model, nothing automatically forces them together.

The Recessionary Expenditure Gap

A recessionary expenditure gap occurs when aggregate expenditures at full-employment GDP fall short of the amount needed to actually buy up full-employment output.

  • Equilibrium GDP settles below potential GDP, a negative GDP gap.
  • The size of the gap is measured as the vertical distance between the AE line and the 45-degree line, at the full-employment GDP level.
  • Because of the multiplier, closing this gap requires less new spending than the size of the output shortfall itself: $ = $ the increase in equilibrium GDP needed.

Example: A Recessionary Gap

Continuing the open economy with \(X_n = +20\) (AE \(= C + I_g + G + X_n\), equilibrium GDP \(= 600\)), suppose this economy’s full-employment GDP is \(750\).

Using the AE schedule: at GDP \(= 750\), \(C = 620\), \(I_g = 40\), \(G = 40\), \(X_n = 20\).

  1. Compute AE at GDP \(= 750\).
  2. What is the size of the recessionary expenditure gap?
  3. Using the multiplier of 5, how much would AE need to rise (at every GDP level) to move equilibrium GDP all the way to 750?

Solution: A Recessionary Gap

AE at GDP \(= 750\): \(620 + 40 + 40 + 20 = 720\).

Recessionary gap: \(750 - 720 = 30\).

Required AE increase: \(30 \times 5 = 150\). Equilibrium GDP would rise from \(600\) to \(600 + 150 = 750\), exactly closing the gap.

The Inflationary Expenditure Gap

The mirror-image case: an inflationary expenditure gap occurs when aggregate expenditures at full-employment GDP exceed the amount needed to buy up full-employment output.

  • Spending is trying to pull the economy above full-employment output.
  • In this simple fixed-price model, that extra spending cannot actually raise real output any further, resources are already fully employed.
  • Instead, once prices are no longer sticky, this excess spending shows up as inflationary pressure rather than more real output.

Treat this as a discussion point rather than a full second graph: the AE model’s fixed-price assumption is exactly what breaks down when spending tries to push past full employment.

Discussion: Gaps and Fiscal Policy

  2. If an economy has an inflationary gap, what general direction would policy need to push AE instead?

Wrap Up

  • The AE model assumes sticky prices, so changes in spending move real output rather than prices, in the short run. Keynes challenged Say’s Law by arguing spending is not guaranteed to buy up full-employment output.
  • In a private closed economy, \(AE = C + I_g\). Equilibrium GDP occurs where AE crosses the 45-degree line, where planned spending equals output.
  • Above equilibrium, unplanned inventories build up and firms cut production. Below equilibrium, inventories are drawn down and firms raise production.
  • Adding \(G\) shifts AE up and raises equilibrium GDP through the multiplier; a tax shifts AE down by \(\text{MPC} \times T\), less than the tax itself.
  • Adding net exports gives the full model, \(AE = C + I_g + G + X_n\), linking our equilibrium GDP to conditions abroad.
  • A recessionary gap pulls equilibrium GDP below full-employment GDP; an inflationary gap pulls spending above what full-employment output can absorb.

Looking ahead: Chapter 12 lets the price level move with the AD-AS model, and Chapter 13 covers the fiscal policy tools that can help close the expenditure gaps introduced here.
