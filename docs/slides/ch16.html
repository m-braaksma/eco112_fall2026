Economic Investment vs. Financial Investment

Recap: Economic Investment (Chapter 6)

Chapter 6 defined economic investment as spending on new capital goods: machinery, buildings, equipment, anything that adds to the economy’s productive capacity.

  • Economic investment is counted in GDP. It creates new capital that did not exist before.
  • This chapter introduces a second, very different meaning of “investment,” the one people usually mean in everyday conversation.

Financial Investment

A financial investment is the purchase of an existing asset, a stock, a bond, a piece of real estate, using money you already have.

  • A financial investment is an exchange of one asset for another: you hand over cash, someone else hands over a stock certificate or a deed.
  • By itself, a financial investment creates no new capital. Ownership of something that already existed simply changes hands.
  • Financial investment is not counted directly in GDP, only economic investment is.

Same word, two very different ideas. Keeping them separate is the whole point of this section.

Where the Two Can Connect

Financial investment and economic investment are not unrelated, they can feed into each other through the market for loanable funds (Chapter 6).

  • Household saving flows into financial markets, where it can be lent to a firm that then uses it for economic investment in new capital.
  • But the financial transaction itself, buying a share, buying a bond, is a different event from the economic investment it may eventually help finance.

A financial investment can help fund economic investment down the line, but the two are not the same act.

Example: Classify Each Scenario

For each scenario, classify it as economic investment, financial investment, both, or neither.

  1. A city issues new bonds to raise money, and uses that money to build a new bridge.
  2. A person buys shares of an existing, publicly traded company on the stock market.
  3. A firm buys a brand-new machine for its factory floor.
  4. A person buys an existing rental property from its previous owner.

Solution: Classify Each Scenario

  1. Both. Issuing the bond is a financial transaction, but the money raised is then spent on a new capital good, the bridge, which is economic investment.
  1. Financial investment only. The shares already existed, ownership just changes hands, no new capital is created.
  1. Economic investment only. A new capital good is purchased directly, no existing asset changes hands.
  1. Financial investment only. The property already existed; buying it does not add any new capital to the economy, even though it may feel like “investing.”

Present Value and Future Value

A Dollar Today vs. a Dollar Tomorrow

Money available today is worth more than the same dollar amount available in the future, because money today can be put to work earning interest.

  • Give up a dollar today, and by next year it could have grown into more than a dollar, if invested at some interest rate.
  • This simple fact is the foundation for pricing almost every financial asset: a stock, a bond, even a lottery payout.

Future Value

Future value is what a sum of money available today will grow into after earning interest for some number of years.

\[X_t = (1+i)^t X_0\]

  • \(X_0\): the amount you have today
  • \(i\): the interest rate (per year)
  • \(t\): the number of years
  • \(X_t\): the value after \(t\) years of compounding

Connecting Back: The Rule of 70 (Chapter 8)

Chapter 8 introduced the Rule of 70: at growth rate \(g\) percent per year, a quantity doubles in roughly \(70/g\) years.

  • That is exactly the same compounding logic as the future value formula, just turned into a quick mental shortcut instead of a full calculation.
  • At 5 percent interest, a sum roughly doubles in about \(70/5 = 14\) years, without needing to solve \((1.05)^t = 2\) directly.

Two tools for the same idea: the future value formula gives the exact number, the Rule of 70 gives a fast estimate.

Example: Future Value

You have $1,000 today. The interest rate is 5 percent per year.

  1. How much will you have after 3 years, using the future value formula?
  2. Using the Rule of 70, roughly how many years would it take for this $1,000 to double?

Solution: Future Value

  1. \(X_3 = (1.05)^3 \times 1{,}000 = 1{,}157.63\) (illustrative).
  1. Rule of 70: \(70 / 5 = 14\) years. Consistent with the exact formula, just a faster estimate for the doubling question specifically.

Present Value

Present value is the same relationship solved the other way: how much a future sum of money is worth today, given some interest rate.

\[X_0 = \frac{X_t}{(1+i)^t}\]

  • This is called discounting the future amount back to today’s dollars.
  • A rational person should be indifferent between receiving \(X_0\) today and receiving \(X_t\) in \(t\) years, since \(X_0\) invested today at rate \(i\) grows into exactly \(X_t\).

Example: Present Value

You are promised $1,000 (illustrative), paid to you in exactly 3 years. The interest rate is 5 percent.

  1. What is the present value of that promise?
  2. Explain, using the future value formula, why a rational person should be indifferent between that present value today and the $1,000 in 3 years.

Solution: Present Value

  1. \(X_0 = 1{,}000 / (1.05)^3 = 863.84\) (illustrative).
  1. Invest $863.84 today at 5 percent for 3 years: \(863.84 \times (1.05)^3 = 1{,}000\). The two amounts grow into each other, so a rational person should value them equally.

Comparing Payment Streams with Present Value

Different Schedules, Same Value

Two assets can pay out different dollar amounts on different schedules, and still be worth exactly the same amount today, once each payment is discounted back to the present.

  • This is exactly how buyers compare very different-looking financial assets: a bond with one payment, a stock with several dividend payments, an annuity with many small payments.
  • Reduce every stream to a single present value number, then compare that number directly.

Whichever stream has the higher total present value is worth more today, regardless of how the payments are timed.

Example: Compare Two Payment Streams

The interest rate is 10 percent. Compare two hypothetical assets:

  • Asset A pays $550 one year from now, and $605 two years from now.
  • Asset B pays $1,100 one year from now, and nothing after that.
  1. Compute the present value of Asset A (sum the present value of each payment).
  2. Compute the present value of Asset B.
  3. Which asset is worth more today, if at all?

Solution: Compare Two Payment Streams

  1. Asset A: \(550/(1.10) + 605/(1.10)^2 = 500 + 500 = 1{,}000\).
  1. Asset B: \(1{,}100/(1.10) = 1{,}000\).
  1. They are worth exactly the same today, $1,000 each, even though the payment schedules look completely different. Present value is what lets you compare them on equal footing.

Harder Problem: A Third Payment Stream

The interest rate is still 10 percent. A third hypothetical asset, Asset C, pays $330 one year from now, $363 two years from now, and $399.30 three years from now.

  1. Compute the present value of each of the three payments.
  2. Compute the total present value of Asset C.
  3. Compared to Assets A and B above (each worth $1,000 today), is Asset C worth more, less, or the same today?

Work through this individually or with a partner before checking the next slide.

Review: A Third Payment Stream

  1. \(330/(1.10) = 300\), \(\quad 363/(1.10)^2 = 300\), \(\quad 399.30/(1.10)^3 = 300\).
  1. Total present value \(= 300 + 300 + 300 = 900\).
  1. Asset C is worth less today, $900 versus $1,000 for Assets A and B, even though it pays out over more years. Present value, not the number of payments, is what determines an asset’s worth today.

Rate of Return on a Stock

The Simple Dividend Yield

For a simple case, ignoring any change in the stock’s price, a stock’s rate of return is its annual dividend payment divided by its purchase price.

\[\text{Rate of return} = \frac{\text{Annual dividend payment}}{\text{Purchase price}}\]

  • This is often called the dividend yield.
  • It lets you compare very different stocks, priced very differently, on the same simple percentage scale.

Example: Comparing Dividend Yields

Stock X pays an annual dividend of $2 per share, and currently sells for $40 per share (illustrative).

  1. What is Stock X’s rate of return?
  2. Stock Y pays an annual dividend of $4 per share, and currently sells for $50 per share. What is Stock Y’s rate of return?
  3. Which stock currently offers the higher return?

Solution: Comparing Dividend Yields

  1. Stock X: \(2 / 40 = 0.05\), or 5 percent.
  1. Stock Y: \(4 / 50 = 0.08\), or 8 percent.
  1. Stock Y offers the higher return, 8 percent versus 5 percent.

Why Returns Tend to Equalize

If Stock Y offers a clearly higher return than Stock X, why doesn’t everyone just hold Stock Y forever?

  • Investors sell the lower-return stock (Stock X) to buy the higher-return one (Stock Y).
  • Selling Stock X pushes its price down, which, with its dividend unchanged, raises its rate of return.
  • Buying Stock Y pushes its price up, which, with its dividend unchanged, lowers its rate of return.
  • This buying and selling continues until returns on similarly risky assets converge toward each other.

This is the basic arbitrage intuition: large, persistent, riskless differences in return rarely last, because investors act on them.

Discussion: Return Convergence

  1. If Stock X’s price falls enough that its return rises to match Stock Y’s, would investors still have a reason to keep selling Stock X?
  2. Why might two stocks not fully converge to the same return, even after this process plays out?

Hint for question 2: think about what else might differ between the two companies besides the dividend.

Diversification and Risk

Two Kinds of Risk

Not all investment risk is the same. Economists separate it into two categories.

  • Diversifiable risk (also called idiosyncratic risk): risk specific to one company or one asset, a factory fire, a bad product launch, a scandal at a single firm.
  • Nondiversifiable risk (also called systemic risk): risk that affects the whole market or the whole economy at once, a recession, a broad spike in interest rates, a pandemic.

The difference matters enormously for how an investor should try to manage risk.

Diversification

Diversification means holding many different assets rather than just one or two, so that one asset’s bad luck does not sink your entire portfolio.

  • Diversifiable risk can be reduced, even eliminated, by holding a wide enough mix of assets: one company’s bad news is offset by another company’s good news.
  • Nondiversifiable risk cannot be eliminated this way. If the whole market falls, holding many different stocks does not protect you, they tend to fall together.

Diversification is a free lunch against one kind of risk, and no help at all against the other.

Mutual Funds and Index Funds

Most ordinary investors diversify without picking individual stocks themselves, through mutual funds, pooled investment vehicles that hold many different assets on investors’ behalf.

  • An actively managed fund has a manager who researches and picks specific stocks or bonds, trying to outperform the broader market, typically at a higher fee.
  • An index fund is a passively managed fund that simply holds a broad basket of assets meant to track an entire market index, at a much lower fee, without trying to pick winners.

Both are ways of achieving diversification without buying dozens of individual stocks one at a time.

Example: Classify the Risk

For each event, classify the risk it represents as diversifiable or nondiversifiable.

  1. A single company recalls its flagship product after a manufacturing defect.
  2. A nationwide recession sharply reduces consumer spending across nearly every industry.
  3. A CEO is forced to resign after a scandal at one specific firm.
  4. Interest rates rise sharply across the entire economy, pulling down asset prices broadly.

Solution: Classify the Risk

  1. Diversifiable. Specific to one company, holding many stocks would cushion this loss.
  1. Nondiversifiable. Affects nearly the whole economy at once, diversification across stocks does not protect against it.
  1. Diversifiable. Specific to one firm’s leadership, unrelated to the broader market.
  1. Nondiversifiable. A broad, economy-wide shift in interest rates affects asset prices across the board.

Real Data: The S&P 500

  1. This index tracks 500 large U.S. companies at once. Is a sharp drop in this index more likely diversifiable or nondiversifiable risk?

Answer: nondiversifiable, a broad index falling means most of the market is falling together, which diversification across stocks cannot protect against.

  1. Why might a beginning investor choose an index fund that tracks this series, rather than picking individual stocks?

Answer: it offers instant diversification against company-specific risk at a low fee, without needing to research and pick winners individually.

  1. Does a period where this index rises steadily mean every individual stock in it also rose? Why or why not?

Answer: not necessarily, the index is a broad average; individual companies can still face diversifiable, company-specific setbacks even while the overall index climbs.

Bond Risk and Interest Rates

Recap: Bond Prices and Interest Rates (Chapter 14)

Chapter 14 established the inverse relationship between a bond’s price and its interest rate (yield): pay less for the same future payment, and the effective interest rate you earn is higher.

  • This chapter adds a second question: why do different bonds pay different interest rates in the first place?
  • Two big factors: default risk and time to maturity.

Default Risk: Government vs. Corporate Bonds

Default risk is the risk that the borrower fails to pay back the loan.

  • Government bonds, especially from a stable government, typically carry very low default risk, so they typically pay lower interest rates.
  • Corporate bonds carry more default risk, since a company can go bankrupt in a way a national government (with taxing power) is far less likely to, so they typically pay higher interest rates to compensate investors for that extra risk.

Higher risk of not getting paid back means investors demand a higher rate to be willing to lend at all.

Maturity: Short-Term vs. Long-Term Bonds

Holding risk equal, longer-term bonds typically pay higher interest rates than shorter-term bonds.

  • Tying up your money for longer means giving up access to it, and the ability to react to changing conditions, for a longer stretch of time.
  • There is also more uncertainty further into the future, about inflation, about the economy, about the borrower itself, and investors demand compensation for that added uncertainty.

Same logic as the time preference* idea from earlier in the course: waiting longer requires a bigger reward.*

Example: Rank the Bonds

Consider three hypothetical bonds:

  • Bond 1: a 5-year U.S. Treasury bond.
  • Bond 2: a 5-year corporate bond, same maturity as Bond 1, issued by a private company.
  • Bond 3: a 20-year U.S. Treasury bond, same issuer as Bond 1, longer maturity.
  1. Compare Bond 1 and Bond 2. Which should pay the higher interest rate, and why?
  2. Compare Bond 1 and Bond 3. Which should pay the higher interest rate, and why?

Solution: Rank the Bonds

  1. Bond 2 (the corporate bond) should pay the higher rate. Same maturity, but Bond 2 carries more default risk than a government bond.
  1. Bond 3 (the 20-year Treasury) should pay the higher rate. Same issuer and default risk as Bond 1, but Bond 3 ties up an investor’s money for much longer, requiring extra compensation.

Note: comparing Bond 2 and Bond 3 directly is trickier, since default risk and maturity are both different between them, that is exactly why real-world bond pricing isolates one factor at a time.

Real Data: The 10-Year Treasury Rate

  1. Since this is a government bond, why does it pay any interest at all, if government default risk is very low?

Answer: even with low default risk, this is a long-maturity bond, so its rate still reflects time preference and uncertainty about inflation and conditions over 10 years.

  1. Would you expect a corporate bond of the same 10-year maturity to pay more or less than this series, and why?

Answer: more, a same-maturity corporate bond adds default risk on top of the maturity premium already reflected here.

  1. If this rate rises sharply, what would you expect to happen to existing bond prices, based on Chapter 14?

Answer: existing bond prices fall, the inverse relationship between bond prices and interest rates from Chapter 14.
