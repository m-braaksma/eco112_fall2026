From a Fixed Price Level to a Model That Lets Prices Move

Connecting Back: The AE Model’s Fixed-Price Assumption (Chapter 11)

Chapter 11 built the aggregate expenditures (AE) model on one simplifying assumption: the price level is fixed. Changes in spending showed up entirely as changes in real output.

  • Only the vertical position of the AE line could shift.
  • Equilibrium was wherever AE crossed the 45-degree line, no price level in sight.

What’s missing: what happens to the price level itself when spending changes?

Introducing the AD-AS Model

The aggregate demand and aggregate supply (AD-AS) model graphs the price level against real GDP for the economy as a whole.

  • Vertical axis: the price level (an overall index of prices, not the price of one good).
  • Horizontal axis: real GDP (total output of the whole economy).
  • Now both axes can move, equilibrium is a price level and a GDP level together.

This looks like a single-market supply and demand graph from Chapter 3, but it isn’t the same thing: Chapter 3 describes one good (corn, laptops); AD-AS describes the whole economy at once, and the reasons behind the slopes are completely different, as you’ll see next.

Aggregate Demand

What Is Aggregate Demand?

Aggregate demand (AD) is a curve showing the total quantity of real GDP that all buyers in the economy, households, businesses, government, and foreign buyers, are willing to purchase at each possible price level.

  • The AD curve slopes downward: as the price level falls, the quantity of real GDP demanded rises, and vice versa.
  • Do not explain this slope with Chapter 3’s income and substitution effects. Those apply to a single good with a fixed budget. AD covers the whole economy’s spending, and needs three different explanations.

Why AD Slopes Downward: Three Reasons

A higher price level reduces the quantity of real GDP demanded for three distinct reasons:

  1. The real-balances effect
  2. The interest-rate effect
  3. The foreign purchases effect

Each one describes a different channel through which a change in the price level itself affects total spending. Next, we take each in turn.

Reason 1: The Real-Balances Effect

Households hold some wealth in fixed-dollar assets, savings accounts, cash, bonds.

  • When the price level rises, each dollar of that wealth buys less. People feel poorer in real terms, even though the dollar amount hasn’t changed.
  • Feeling poorer, households cut back on spending.
  • A higher price level therefore reduces the real value of wealth, reducing the quantity of real GDP demanded, this is the real-balances effect.

Reason 2: The Interest-Rate Effect

Recall Chapter 10’s investment demand curve: lower interest rates encourage more investment spending.

  • A higher price level means people and businesses need to hold more money to cover the same transactions (everything costs more).
  • Higher demand for money pushes up interest rates.
  • Higher interest rates discourage interest-sensitive spending, especially business investment, but also things like car and home purchases.
  • A higher price level therefore raises interest rates and reduces the quantity of real GDP demanded, this is the interest-rate effect.

Reason 3: The Foreign Purchases Effect

Recall Chapter 11’s net exports (\(X_n\) = exports minus imports).

  • If the domestic price level rises while prices abroad stay the same, domestic goods become relatively more expensive than foreign goods.
  • Foreign buyers purchase fewer of our exports, and domestic buyers purchase more imports instead.
  • Net exports fall.
  • A higher price level therefore reduces net exports and reduces the quantity of real GDP demanded, this is the foreign purchases effect.

Example: Which Effect Is It?

For each scenario, identify whether it illustrates the real-balances effect, the interest-rate effect, or the foreign purchases effect.

  1. The price level rises, and a family notices their savings account doesn’t stretch as far as it used to, so they postpone a kitchen remodel.

Real-balances effect: fixed-dollar wealth (the savings account) buys less in real terms, so spending falls.

  1. The price level rises, pushing up the interest rate on business loans, so a manufacturer delays buying a new machine.

Interest-rate effect: the higher price level raised money demand and interest rates, which discouraged interest-sensitive investment spending.

  1. The price level rises here at home while prices in a major trading partner stay flat, and export orders for domestically made furniture decline.

Foreign purchases effect: relatively higher domestic prices made our goods less attractive abroad, reducing exports and net exports.

What Shifts Aggregate Demand

Movement Along AD vs. a Shift in AD

It’s important to separate two different things that can change real GDP demanded:

  • A change in the price level itself causes a movement along a fixed AD curve (the three effects just covered).
  • A change in any other factor that affects total spending causes the entire AD curve to shift, right (an increase in AD) or left (a decrease in AD).

Same AD curve: a price-level change (A to B) moves you along it.

A non-price factor (confidence, \(G\), exchange rates…) moves the whole curve.

The Determinants of AD

At any given price level, AD shifts when one of its spending components changes:

  • Consumption: household wealth, borrowing, expectations about the future, or taxes (recall Chapter 10).
  • Investment: business confidence, interest rates, or new technology (recall Chapter 10).
  • Government spending: a direct policy choice.
  • Net exports: foreign income or exchange rates (recall Chapter 11).

An increase in any of these shifts AD right. A decrease in any of these shifts AD left.

AD Shifts and the Multiplier

Recall the multiplier from Chapters 10 and 11: an initial change in spending gets re-spent through the economy, producing a larger final change in total spending.

  • The same logic applies here. An initial change in \(C\), \(I_g\), \(G\), or \(X_n\) does not just shift AD by its own size, it shifts AD (measured in real GDP, at a given price level) by that change times the multiplier.
  • Multiplier \(= 1/\text{MPS} = 1/(1-\text{MPC})\), same formula as before.

The AD curve is really a snapshot of the AE model’s equilibrium at each possible price level, so the multiplier carries over directly.

Example: Shifting AD with the Multiplier

In this economy, the marginal propensity to consume is \(\text{MPC} = 0.75\). The government increases spending by \(\Delta G = 30\).

  1. What is the multiplier?

Multiplier \(= 1/(1-0.75) = 1/0.25 = 4\).

  1. By how much does the AD curve shift, in terms of real GDP, at a given price level?

AD shifts by \(\Delta G \times \text{multiplier} = 30 \times 4 = 120\). At any given price level, the quantity of real GDP demanded rises by 120.

  1. Does AD shift right or left?

AD shifts right (an increase), since government spending rose.

Aggregate Supply: Short Run and Long Run

Why Economists Added a Supply Side

The AD curve alone was the whole model for decades. In the 1970s, oil shocks (1973, 1979) pushed up production costs economy-wide, and something unprecedented happened:

  • Prices rose, but so did unemployment, both at once. A demand-only model couldn’t produce that combination at all.
  • Economists called it stagflation: stagnation and inflation together.

This gap between the model and the data is what pushed economists to add an explicit supply side, one that could shift on its own, separate from demand.

Two Aggregate Supply Curves

Unlike AD, aggregate supply (AS) is described by two different curves, depending on the time horizon:

  • The short-run aggregate supply curve (SRAS): upward-sloping.
  • The long-run aggregate supply curve (LRAS): vertical.

This distinction matters a great deal for understanding inflation, so we cover each carefully before combining them with AD.

Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS)

Recall Chapter 6’s sticky prices: in the short run, many input prices, especially wages, are slow to adjust.

  • If the output price level rises while wages and other input costs stay temporarily fixed, firms’ profit margins widen.
  • Wider margins encourage firms to produce and sell more.
  • This gives the SRAS curve its upward slope: a higher price level, holding input prices fixed, brings a higher quantity of real GDP supplied.

SRAS describes a time horizon short enough that input prices haven’t caught up yet.

Long-Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS)

In the long run, wages and other input prices fully adjust to changes in the price level.

  • If the price level rises and wages eventually rise right along with it, firms’ profit margins go back to normal, there’s no longer any incentive to produce more.
  • The price level, by itself, cannot permanently change how much the economy produces.
  • What can change long-run output is the quantity and quality of resources and technology, the growth factors from Chapter 8 (labor, capital, natural resources, productivity).

The LRAS curve is a vertical line at the economy’s full-employment (potential) GDP, the level of output the economy can sustain once all input prices have adjusted.

SRAS and LRAS Together

SRAS slopes up because input prices are temporarily sticky. LRAS is vertical because in the long run, only real resources and technology, not the price level, determine potential output.

Example: SRAS or LRAS Reasoning?

For each statement, decide whether the reasoning belongs to the short-run (SRAS) or long-run (LRAS) view of aggregate supply.

  1. “Wage contracts are still in effect for the next several months, so a higher price level right now lets firms earn wider margins and produce more.”

SRAS: sticky wage contracts mean input costs lag behind the price level.

  1. “Once all wage contracts have renegotiated to catch up with prices, output settles back at the level determined by labor, capital, and technology.”

LRAS: once input prices fully adjust, output returns to potential, independent of the price level.

Example: SRAS or LRAS Reasoning?

For each statement, decide whether the reasoning belongs to the short-run (SRAS) or long-run (LRAS) view of aggregate supply.

  1. “An economy’s maximum sustainable output depends on how much it can produce with its resources, not on where the price level happens to sit.”

LRAS: this is the growth-factor logic behind the vertical curve.

  1. “A retailer’s supplier hasn’t raised wholesale prices yet, so a price increase this month is pure extra profit that encourages more restocking.”

SRAS: a temporary gap between output prices and input costs is exactly the short-run mechanism.

What Shifts Aggregate Supply

The Determinants of AS

Both SRAS and LRAS can shift when the underlying conditions of production change (not just the price level, which causes a movement along SRAS):

  • Input prices: the cost of labor, energy, or imported materials (recall Chapter 10’s discussion of production costs).
  • Productivity: output per unit of input; recall Chapter 8’s growth accounting, higher productivity lets the economy produce more from the same resources.
  • The legal-institutional environment: business taxes and subsidies, and the burden of government regulation.

An increase in productivity, or a fall in costs/taxes/regulation, shifts AS right (increase). The reverse shifts AS left (decrease). A shift in productivity or input prices can shift SRAS, LRAS, or both, depending on whether the change is temporary or affects the economy’s long-run capacity.

Example: Which Way Does AS Shift?

For each scenario, determine whether aggregate supply shifts right (increase) or left (decrease).

  1. A new manufacturing technology raises productivity economy-wide.

Right: higher productivity means more output from the same resources.

  1. The price of a key imported input, such as oil, spikes sharply.

Left: higher input costs raise production costs and reduce the quantity supplied at every price level.

Example: Which Way Does AS Shift?

For each scenario, determine whether aggregate supply shifts right (increase) or left (decrease).

  1. Business taxes are cut across the economy.

Right: lower taxes reduce the cost of doing business, encouraging more production.

  1. The government adds a costly new layer of regulatory compliance for firms.

Left: added regulatory burden raises the cost of producing, reducing the quantity supplied.

Putting AD and AS Together

Long-Run Equilibrium

Equilibrium in the AD-AS model occurs where the AD curve intersects the SRAS curve, determining the equilibrium price level and equilibrium real GDP. In long-run equilibrium, that intersection also sits exactly on the LRAS line, at potential GDP.

Demand-Pull Inflation: Setup

Demand-pull inflation occurs when an increase in AD (a rightward shift) pushes the economy up along SRAS.

  • The equilibrium price level rises.
  • Equilibrium real GDP rises too, moving together with the price level.
  • If AD rises far enough, real GDP can move temporarily above potential GDP (LRAS), an inflationary gap, echoing the inflationary expenditure gap from Chapter 11, and consistent with Chapter 9’s definition of inflation as a rising price level.

Example: Demand-Pull Inflation

Starting from the long-run equilibrium above (price level \(100\), real GDP \(550\), potential GDP \(550\)), consumer confidence rises and AD increases by 100 (in real GDP terms) at every price level.

  1. Using the SRAS curve, where does the new equilibrium price level and real GDP land?
  2. Is real GDP now above, below, or equal to potential GDP?
  3. What do we call the situation where real GDP exceeds potential GDP?

Solution: Demand-Pull Inflation

New equilibrium: price level 110, real GDP 600.

Real GDP (600) is above potential GDP (550) by 50.

That gap is an inflationary gap: the economy is producing beyond what it can sustain once input prices catch up.

Cost-Push Inflation and Stagflation: Setup

Cost-push inflation occurs when a decrease in SRAS (a leftward shift), for example from a spike in input costs, raises the price level.

  • The equilibrium price level rises, just like demand-pull inflation.
  • But equilibrium real GDP falls, the opposite direction from demand-pull inflation.
  • Rising prices combined with falling real output is called stagflation, a difficult combination for policymakers because there is no single AD shift that fixes both problems at once.

Contrast this directly with demand-pull inflation: there, real GDP moved with the price level. Here, real GDP moves opposite the price level.

Example: Cost-Push Inflation

Starting again from the original long-run equilibrium (price level \(100\), real GDP \(550\)), a spike in imported input costs shifts SRAS left, reducing the quantity of real GDP supplied by 100 at every price level.

  1. Using the AD curve (unchanged), where does the new equilibrium price level and real GDP land?
  2. Compare the direction of the price level change to the direction of the real GDP change.
  3. What is this combination called?

Solution: Cost-Push Inflation

New equilibrium: price level 110, real GDP 500.

Price level rose (100 to 110) while real GDP fell (550 to 500), a recessionary gap of 50 combined with rising prices.

This is stagflation: stagnating output and inflation at the same time.

The Reverse Case: A Decrease in AD

For completeness, a decrease in AD (leftward shift) produces the opposite of demand-pull inflation:

  • The equilibrium price level falls.
  • Equilibrium real GDP falls below potential GDP, a recessionary gap, the same concept as Chapter 11’s recessionary expenditure gap, now visible as a fall in the price level too, not just output.

Chapter 11 already covered the intuition behind a recessionary gap. This is simply that same gap, now shown on the AD-AS diagram where the price level can also adjust.

Summary: Four Directional Cases

Shift Price level Real GDP Name
AD increases (shifts right) Rises Rises (possibly above potential) Demand-pull inflation
AD decreases (shifts left) Falls Falls (below potential) Recessionary gap
SRAS decreases (shifts left) Rises Falls (below potential) Cost-push inflation / stagflation
SRAS increases (shifts right) Falls Rises Favorable supply shift

Notice the key contrast: an AD shift moves the price level and real GDP in the same direction. An SRAS shift moves them in opposite directions.

Wrap Up

  • The AD-AS model replaces Chapter 11’s fixed price level, plotting price level against real GDP for the whole economy.
  • AD slopes downward because of the real-balances effect, the interest-rate effect, and the foreign purchases effect. It shifts with changes in \(C\), \(I_g\), \(G\), or \(X_n\), magnified by the multiplier.
  • SRAS slopes upward because input prices, especially wages, are sticky in the short run. LRAS is vertical at potential GDP, since in the long run only resources and technology, not the price level, determine output.
  • AS shifts with input prices, productivity, and the legal-institutional environment.
  • Demand-pull inflation: AD increases, price level and real GDP rise together, possibly creating an inflationary gap.
  • Cost-push inflation / stagflation: SRAS decreases, price level rises while real GDP falls, the opposite pattern from demand-pull inflation.
  • A decrease in AD produces the mirror image of demand-pull inflation: falling price level and a recessionary gap.
