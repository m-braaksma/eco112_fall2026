Practice Problems

Warm-Up: Quick Concept and Calculation Checks

  1. If real GDP is temporarily above potential GDP, what eventually happens to nominal wages, and which direction does SRAS shift as a result?
  2. If real GDP is temporarily below potential GDP, what eventually happens to nominal wages (even though this happens slowly), and which direction does SRAS shift as a result?
  3. On the short-run Phillips curve, is the relationship between unemployment and inflation positive or negative?
  4. Is the long-run Phillips curve upward sloping, downward sloping, or vertical? At what unemployment rate does it sit?
  5. If unemployment is temporarily below the natural rate and inflation is rising, is this a short-run or a long-run Phillips curve situation?
  6. Why does the short-run unemployment-inflation tradeoff eventually disappear as expected inflation catches up to actual inflation?
Show Answer
  1. Nominal wages eventually rise (firms compete for workers in a tight labor market), which shifts SRAS left.
  2. Nominal wages eventually fall, though slowly since wages are sticky downward, which shifts SRAS right.
  3. Negative (inverse): lower unemployment tends to come with higher inflation, and vice versa.
  4. Vertical, at the natural rate of unemployment.
  5. Short-run. The long run is defined by the economy having fully adjusted back to the natural rate of unemployment, regardless of the inflation rate.
  6. Once expected inflation rises to match actual inflation, the short-run Phillips curve itself shifts, and the temporary tradeoff vanishes, unemployment drifts back toward the natural rate even if inflation remains elevated.

Problem 1: Self-Correction After an Inflationary Gap

An economy’s potential GDP is 400 (illustrative units). AD is fixed at \(AD(P) = 600 - 4(P-90)\). The original short-run supply curve is \(SRAS_0(P) = 400 + 4(P-90)\).

  1. Find the short-run equilibrium price level and real GDP where \(AD(P) = SRAS_0(P)\).
  2. Is this equilibrium’s real GDP above, below, or equal to potential GDP? What is this gap called?
  3. Over time, self-correction shifts the supply curve to \(SRAS_1(P) = 200 + 4(P-90)\). Find the new equilibrium where \(AD(P) = SRAS_1(P)\).
  4. Compare the new equilibrium’s real GDP to potential GDP. What happened to the price level between the two equilibria?
Show Answer
  1. Setting \(600-4(P-90)=400+4(P-90)\): \(200=8(P-90) \Rightarrow P-90=25 \Rightarrow P=115\). Real GDP \(= 400+4(25)=500\).
  2. Real GDP (500) is above potential GDP (400). This is an inflationary gap.
  3. Setting \(600-4(P-90)=200+4(P-90)\): \(400=8(P-90) \Rightarrow P-90=50 \Rightarrow P=140\). Real GDP \(=200+4(50)=400\).
  4. The new equilibrium’s real GDP (400) now exactly equals potential GDP, the economy has fully self-corrected. The price level rose, from 115 to 140, and settled permanently higher, exactly the pattern predicted: an inflationary gap closes through rising nominal wages, shrinking SRAS, and a higher long-run price level, not through any change in long-run output.

Problem 2: Reading the Short-Run Phillips Curve

A short-run Phillips curve is given by the relationship: inflation rate \(= 10 - u\), where \(u\) is the unemployment rate (in percent). The natural rate of unemployment in this economy is 5 percent.

  1. What is the inflation rate when unemployment is 4 percent?
  2. What is the inflation rate when unemployment is 8 percent?
  3. What is the inflation rate when unemployment is exactly at the natural rate (5 percent)?
  4. Explain why this same short-run Phillips curve is really just another way of drawing the demand-pull side of the AD/AS model from Chapter 12.
Show Answer
  1. Inflation \(= 10 - 4 = 6\) percent.
  2. Inflation \(= 10 - 8 = 2\) percent.
  3. Inflation \(= 10 - 5 = 5\) percent.
  4. Higher AD (demand-pull inflation) both raises the price level and raises real GDP, which means more hiring and lower unemployment. The same underlying demand-pull story that raises the price level and real GDP together in the AD/AS model also raises inflation while lowering unemployment, just plotted on different axes.

Problem 3: When Expected Inflation Rises

A short-run Phillips curve is \(\pi = \pi^e - (u - 5)\), where \(\pi\) is actual inflation, \(u\) is the unemployment rate (in percent), \(\pi^e\) is expected inflation, and 5 percent is the natural rate of unemployment.

  1. If expected inflation \(\pi^e = 3\) percent, what is actual inflation when unemployment is exactly at the natural rate (5 percent)?
  2. Still with \(\pi^e = 3\) percent, suppose the economy overheats and unemployment falls to 3 percent. What is actual inflation now?
  3. Now suppose expected inflation has risen to \(\pi^e = 6\) percent (people have come to expect more inflation, based on recent experience), with unemployment still at 3 percent. What is actual inflation?
  4. Compare your answers to (b) and (c). What happened to the short-run Phillips curve itself as expected inflation rose? At the natural rate of unemployment, does actual inflation ever differ from expected inflation in this model?
Show Answer
  1. \(\pi = 3 - (5-5) = 3 - 0 = 3\) percent. At the natural rate, the \((u-5)\) term is zero, so actual inflation simply equals expected inflation.
  2. \(\pi = 3 - (3-5) = 3 + 2 = 5\) percent. With unemployment below the natural rate, actual inflation runs above expected inflation, the short-run tradeoff at work.
  3. \(\pi = 6 - (3-5) = 6 + 2 = 8\) percent.
  4. Comparing (b) and (c), at the same unemployment rate (3 percent), actual inflation rose from 5 to 8 percent, exactly the 3 percentage point rise in expected inflation. The entire short-run Phillips curve shifted up by that amount. And at the natural rate of unemployment specifically, actual inflation always exactly equals expected inflation, whatever that happens to be, there is no stable, exploitable tradeoff there: unemployment cannot be held permanently below the natural rate just by tolerating ever-higher inflation, once expectations catch up, only inflation ends up permanently higher, not unemployment permanently lower.
