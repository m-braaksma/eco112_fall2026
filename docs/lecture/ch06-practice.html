Practice Problems

Warm-Up: Quick Concept and Calculation Checks

  1. A town’s population grows from 8,000 to 8,400 in one year. What is the population growth rate?
  2. A country’s real GDP is $50 million and its population is 25,000. What is real GDP per capita?
  3. A household saves $500 per month. How much total saving does that household contribute in one year?
  4. A $10,000 investment is expected to generate $800 per year in profit. What is the expected rate of return?
  5. A retiree buys 10 shares of an existing company’s stock on the stock market. Does this count as Investment in the economic sense? Why or why not?
  6. A bakery purchases a new industrial oven to expand production. Does this count as Investment in the economic sense? Why or why not?
  7. A landlord signs a 12-month apartment lease with tenants at a fixed monthly rent. Is this price sticky or flexible?
  8. The price of Bitcoin on a cryptocurrency exchange. Is this price sticky or flexible?

Problem 1: Total Growth vs. Per-Capita Growth

A country’s real GDP grows from $18.0 trillion to $18.9 trillion over one year. Over that same year, the country’s population grows from 300 million to 306 million people.

  1. What is the percentage growth rate of total real GDP?
  2. What is real GDP per capita in each year?
  3. What is the percentage growth rate of real GDP per capita?
  4. Why is the answer to (c) smaller than the answer to (a)?

Problem 2: Saving, Investment, and Expected Returns

A firm is deciding whether to build a new factory that costs $5 million. The firm forecasts the factory would generate $600,000 in additional profit per year.

  1. What annual rate of return does the firm expect on this $5 million investment?
  2. If interest rates rise and the firm could instead earn a guaranteed 8 percent per year by simply depositing that $5 million in a bank, should the firm still build the factory? Explain using the idea of expected returns.
  3. Explain, in one or two sentences, why a general rise in interest rates throughout the economy tends to reduce the total amount of economic investment.

Problem 3: When the Average Isn’t the Typical Household

A small country has exactly 5 households. Their annual incomes in Year 1 and Year 2 are:

Household Year 1 Income Year 2 Income
1 $30,000 $31,000
2 $32,000 $33,000
3 $35,000 $36,000
4 $38,000 $39,000
5 $65,000 $111,000
  1. Compute the mean (average) household income in Year 1 and in Year 2, and the growth rate of the mean.
  2. Compute the median household income in Year 1 and in Year 2 (the income of the middle household when incomes are sorted low to high), and the growth rate of the median.
  3. A news report says “average income in this country grew 25 percent this year.” Is that statement misleading? Why or why not?
  4. Relate this example back to the GDP per capita versus median household income discussion from the slides. Why might a country with healthy GDP-per-capita growth still have a lot of unhappy citizens?

Problem 4: The Market for Loanable Funds

Chapter 3 introduced supply and demand for goods, like soybeans or textbooks. The same model can describe the market where saving meets investment, called the market for loanable funds. Savers supply funds to this market (depositing money in banks, buying bonds), hoping to earn interest. Borrowers, often firms that want to invest, demand funds from this market, and have to pay interest to get them.

The table below shows the (hypothetical) market for loanable funds in a country.

Interest Rate Quantity of Saving Supplied Quantity of Investment Demanded
2% $100 billion $400 billion
3% $150 billion $350 billion
4% $200 billion $300 billion
5% $250 billion $250 billion
6% $300 billion $200 billion
7% $350 billion $150 billion
  1. Why does it make sense to treat saving as a supply curve and investment as a demand curve in this market? What plays the role of “price”?
  2. Using the table, what is the equilibrium interest rate, and what quantity of funds gets loaned at that rate?
  3. Suppose the interest rate is temporarily stuck at 3 percent. Is there a shortage or surplus of loanable funds at that rate, and how large? Which direction would you expect the interest rate to move to restore equilibrium?
  4. Using what you learned about supply and demand shifts in Chapter 3: if a new government policy encourages people to save more at every interest rate (the saving curve shifts right), what happens to the equilibrium interest rate and the equilibrium quantity of loanable funds?
