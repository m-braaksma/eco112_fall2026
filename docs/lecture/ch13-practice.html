Practice Problems

Warm-Up: Quick Concept and Calculation Checks

  1. To fight a recession, should the government use expansionary or contractionary fiscal policy? Name two specific tools it could use.
  2. To fight demand-pull inflation, should the government use expansionary or contractionary fiscal policy?
  3. True or False: automatic stabilizers require Congress to pass new legislation every time the economy changes.
  4. As GDP rises in an expansion, what automatically happens to tax revenue and to transfer payments, without any new laws being passed?
  5. A country’s actual budget shows a deficit this year, but its cyclically adjusted budget is balanced. What does this combination suggest about whether the deficit was caused by fiscal policy choices or by the state of the economy?
  6. What is the difference between refinancing the public debt and retiring it?
  7. Why might economists consider debt as a percentage of GDP more meaningful than the absolute dollar amount of debt?
Show Answer
  1. Expansionary. Increase government spending (G), decrease taxes (T), or both.
  2. Contractionary. Decrease G, increase T, or both.
  3. False. Automatic stabilizers work without any new legislation, that is exactly what makes them “automatic.”
  4. Tax revenue rises automatically (more income pushes people into higher tax brackets under a progressive system) and transfer payments fall automatically (fewer people qualify for unemployment benefits, for example).
  5. It suggests the deficit was caused mainly by the economy being below full employment (automatic stabilizers responding to a weak economy), not by a deliberately expansionary fiscal policy choice, since at full employment the budget would be balanced under current law.
  6. Refinancing means rolling over the debt, selling new bonds to pay off maturing ones. Retiring means actually paying the debt off, buying back bonds or letting them mature without replacing them.
  7. Debt as a percentage of GDP accounts for the size of the economy that has to service the debt, a large absolute debt is a much smaller burden for a large, wealthy economy than for a small one, so comparing the percentage is more meaningful across time or across countries.

Problem 1: Closing a Recessionary Gap

An economy has a recessionary expenditure gap of $100 billion (aggregate expenditures at full-employment GDP fall $100 billion short of what is needed). The MPC in this economy is 0.8.

  1. What is the multiplier?
  2. How large an increase in government spending (G) alone would close this gap?
  3. How large a tax cut (T) alone would close this gap? (Recall a tax change’s effect is multiplied by MPC first, then by the multiplier.)
  4. Propose one combination of a smaller increase in G together with a tax cut that, added together, would also close the gap exactly.
Show Answer
  1. Multiplier \(= 1/(1-0.8) = 5\).
  2. \(\Delta G\) needed: \(100 / 5 = \$20\) billion.
  3. \(\Delta T\) needed: \(100 / (5 \times 0.8) = \$25\) billion tax cut.
  4. Many combinations work, for example: a \(\$10\) billion increase in G (closes \(10 \times 5 = \$50\) billion of the gap) plus a \(\$12.5\) billion tax cut (closes \(12.5 \times 0.8 \times 5 = \$50\) billion of the gap), together closing the full $100 billion gap. Any pair of G and T changes that add up to $100 billion in combined AD effect works.

Problem 2: The Balanced-Budget Multiplier

The government increases spending by $30 billion and, in the same year, raises taxes by exactly $30 billion to keep the budget balanced. The MPC is 0.8.

  1. Using the multiplier, what is the effect of the $30 billion increase in G alone on equilibrium GDP?
  2. Using the tax multiplier (\(MPC \times\) multiplier), what is the effect of the $30 billion increase in T alone on equilibrium GDP?
  3. Combining (a) and (b), what is the net effect on equilibrium GDP?
  4. Explain in one sentence why an equal-sized increase in G and T does not fully cancel out, even though the government’s budget balance does not change at all.
Show Answer
  1. \(\Delta GDP\) from G: \(30 \times 5 = +\$150\) billion.
  2. \(\Delta GDP\) from T: \(-30 \times 0.8 \times 5 = -\$120\) billion.
  3. Net effect: \(150 - 120 = +\$30\) billion, equilibrium GDP rises by exactly the size of the balanced budget change itself. This is the balanced-budget multiplier: it always equals 1.
  4. Every dollar of new government spending enters the economy in full, but a dollar of new taxes only reduces spending by \(MPC\) of a dollar (households absorb the rest by saving less), so the spending increase always outweighs the tax increase’s contractionary effect.

Problem 3: Automatic Stabilizers, Progressive vs. Flat Taxes

Compare two hypothetical tax systems facing the same household, whose income falls from $60,000 to $50,000 during a downturn:

  • System A (progressive): 10 percent on the first $40,000 of income, 25 percent on income above $40,000.
  • System B (flat): 18 percent on all income, regardless of amount.
  1. Compute this household’s tax bill under System A at $60,000 income and at $50,000 income.
  2. Compute the tax bill under System B at both income levels.
  3. Under which system does the tax bill fall by more when income drops?
  4. Compute disposable (after-tax) income under each system at both income levels, and the drop in disposable income under each. Which system cushions the household’s spending power more?
Show Answer
  1. At $60,000: \(10\% \times 40{,}000 + 25\% \times 20{,}000 = 4{,}000 + 5{,}000 = \$9{,}000\). At $50,000: \(4{,}000 + 25\% \times 10{,}000 = 4{,}000 + 2{,}500 = \$6{,}500\).
  2. At $60,000: \(18\% \times 60{,}000 = \$10{,}800\). At $50,000: \(18\% \times 50{,}000 = \$9{,}000\).
  3. System A’s tax bill falls by \(9{,}000 - 6{,}500 = \$2{,}500\). System B’s falls by only \(10{,}800 - 9{,}000 = \$1{,}800\). The progressive system’s tax revenue falls by more.
  4. Disposable income, System A: \(60{,}000-9{,}000=\$51{,}000\) then \(50{,}000-6{,}500=\$43{,}500\), a drop of $7,500. System B: \(60{,}000-10{,}800=\$49{,}200\) then \(50{,}000-9{,}000=\$41{,}000\), a drop of $8,200. The progressive system (A) cushions the household’s spending power more, its disposable income falls by less, exactly the automatic-stabilizer effect at work.

Problem 4: Accumulating the Public Debt

A small country starts with zero public debt in Year 1. It then runs a deficit of $45 billion in Year 2, a surplus of $10 billion in Year 3, and a deficit of $20 billion in Year 4.

  1. What is the country’s public debt at the end of Year 4?
  2. If this country’s real GDP in Year 4 is $550 billion, what is its public debt as a percentage of GDP?
  3. Suppose most of this debt is held by the country’s own citizens and financial institutions (internally held) rather than by foreign lenders. Briefly explain why economists generally consider this less burdensome than the same debt held externally.
  4. Name one way that financing this debt could crowd out private investment, connecting back to the market for loanable funds from Chapter 6.
Show Answer
  1. Debt accumulates deficits minus surpluses: \(45 - 10 + 20 = \$55\) billion.
  2. \(55 / 550 \times 100 = 10\) percent of GDP.
  3. When debt is held internally, paying interest or principal mostly just transfers purchasing power from one group of the country’s own residents (taxpayers) to another (bondholders), the money stays within the domestic economy. Externally held debt sends that purchasing power abroad instead, which is a real net cost to the country as a whole.
  4. Government borrowing to finance the deficit adds to the demand for loanable funds, which can push up the real interest rate. A higher real interest rate makes fewer private investment projects profitable (recall the investment demand curve from Chapter 10), so some private investment gets “crowded out” by government borrowing.
