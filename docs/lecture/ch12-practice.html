Chapter 12: Aggregate Demand and Aggregate Supply

Practice Problems

Warm-Up: Quick Concept and Calculation Checks

  1. For each scenario, name which of the three reasons the AD curve slopes downward is at work:
    1. a higher price level erodes the purchasing power of household savings accounts, so people spend less; (ii) a higher price level makes domestic goods pricier than foreign alternatives, reducing exports; (iii) a higher price level increases the demand for money, pushing up interest rates and discouraging interest-sensitive investment.
  2. Is the short-run aggregate supply curve (SRAS) upward sloping or vertical? What about the long-run aggregate supply curve (LRAS)?
  3. Which curve, SRAS or LRAS, reflects the idea that input prices (especially wages) are sticky and have not yet adjusted?
  4. A new technology raises productivity across the whole economy. Does this shift AS right or left?
  5. A key imported input suddenly becomes much more expensive. Does this shift AS right or left?
  6. True or False: an increase in AD moves the price level and real GDP in the same direction, while a decrease in AS (a leftward shift) also raises the price level but lowers real GDP.
Show Answer
    1. The real-balances effect. (ii) The foreign purchases effect. (iii) The interest-rate effect.
  2. SRAS is upward sloping. LRAS is vertical, at the economy’s potential (full-employment) level of real GDP.
  3. SRAS. Because input prices have not yet adjusted, a higher output price level raises firms’ profit margins and encourages more production in the short run.
  4. Right. Higher productivity lets firms produce more at every price level.
  5. Left. Higher input costs reduce firms’ profit margins, so they produce less at every price level.
  6. True. This is exactly the contrast between demand-pull inflation (AD shifts, price level and GDP move together) and cost-push inflation (AS shifts, price level and GDP move in opposite directions).

Problem 1: Sizing an AD Shift with the Multiplier

A country’s MPC is 0.8. A wave of business optimism leads firms to increase planned investment spending by $45 billion at every price level.

  1. What is the multiplier?
  2. By how much does this initial change in investment ultimately shift the AD curve (measured in real GDP at a given price level)?
  3. Name one other component of aggregate demand (besides investment) that could shift AD the same way, and briefly explain why.
  4. If MPC had instead been 0.5, would the same $45 billion increase in investment shift AD by more or less than in part (b)? Explain in one sentence.
Show Answer
  1. Multiplier \(= 1/(1-0.8) = 1/0.2 = 5\).
  2. \(\Delta AD = 45 \times 5 = \$225\) billion.
  3. Any of: an increase in government purchases, a tax cut (which raises consumption), or a rise in net exports, all of these add to spending at every price level and get multiplied the same way investment does.
  4. Less. With MPC \(=0.5\), the multiplier is only \(1/(1-0.5)=2\), so the same $45 billion initial change would shift AD by only \(45 \times 2 = \$90\) billion, less than half of part (b)’s $225 billion.

Problem 2: Demand-Pull vs. Cost-Push Inflation

For each event below, determine whether it causes demand-pull or cost-push inflation, and predict what happens to real GDP alongside the price level.

  1. Consumer confidence surges and households sharply increase spending at every price level.
  2. A major hurricane disrupts oil refineries, sharply raising energy costs for nearly every firm in the economy.
  3. The government significantly increases spending on infrastructure projects.
  4. A new tax is imposed on a key raw material used throughout manufacturing.
Show Answer
  1. Demand-pull. Consumer spending directly increases AD, shifting it right, price level rises and real GDP rises together (left panel).
  2. Cost-push. Higher energy costs raise production costs economy-wide, shifting SRAS left, price level rises but real GDP falls (right panel), this combination is stagflation.
  3. Demand-pull. Government purchases directly add to AD.
  4. Cost-push. A tax on a widely used input raises production costs, shifting SRAS left.
