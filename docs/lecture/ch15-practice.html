Practice Problems

Warm-Up: Quick Concept and Calculation Checks

  1. To fight a recession, should the Fed use expansionary or contractionary monetary policy? What happens to interest rates under that policy?
  2. Name the Fed’s key policymaking committee that sets the federal funds rate target.
  3. If the Fed wants to lower interest rates through open market operations, does it buy or sell government bonds? (Recall bond prices and interest rates from Chapter 14.)
  4. What is forward guidance, and how can it affect the economy even before the Fed changes any rate?
  5. When are quantitative easing (QE) and quantitative tightening (QT) typically used, compared to ordinary open market operations?
  6. True or False: monetary policy is equally effective at fighting inflation and at pulling an economy out of a severe recession.
  7. Why does the Fed target roughly 2 percent inflation rather than 0 percent?
Show Answer
  1. Expansionary. Interest rates fall.
  2. The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC).
  3. It buys bonds. Buying bonds bids up bond prices, and since bond prices and interest rates move inversely, this pushes interest rates down.
  4. Forward guidance is the Fed communicating its outlook and future policy intentions. If the Fed is credible, businesses and investors adjust their spending and investment plans immediately based on what the Fed says it intends to do, even before any rate actually changes.
  5. QE and QT are typically used when short-term rates are already very low (near the zero lower bound) and the Fed wants to influence longer-term rates directly, ordinary open market operations mainly target short-term rates.
  6. False. Monetary policy is relatively reliable at slowing an overheating economy, but less reliable at pulling an economy out of a severe recession (cyclical asymmetry and the liquidity trap).
  7. A little inflation lets real wages adjust downward even when nominal wages are sticky and rarely get cut outright, this helps labor markets adjust more smoothly than a 0 percent target would.

Problem 1: Movement Along vs. a Shift in Investment Demand

For each event, determine whether it causes a movement along the investment demand curve or a shift of the entire curve, and explain why.

  1. The Fed actually cuts the federal funds rate target by half a percentage point this week.
  2. The Fed chair gives a speech strongly signaling that rate cuts are likely in the coming months, with no rate change happening yet.
  3. A new technology lowers the cost of building factories across the economy.
  4. Long-term interest rates fall because the Fed begins a new round of quantitative easing.
Show Answer
  1. Movement along the curve. An actual change in the interest rate moves firms along their existing investment demand curve to a new quantity of investment.
  2. Shift of the entire curve (to the right, more investment demanded at every interest rate). No rate has actually changed yet, businesses are adjusting their plans purely because of the credible signal about the future.
  3. Shift of the entire curve (to the right). Lower costs make more projects profitable at every interest rate, this is a change in a non-interest determinant of investment.
  4. Movement along the curve. Even though QE is an unusual tool, it works by actually changing the interest rate (here, a longer-term rate), so it still produces a movement along the existing investment demand curve, not a shift of it.

Problem 2: Tracing the Monetary Policy Transmission Chain

The Fed cuts its target interest rate. As a result, businesses find an additional $30 billion in investment projects newly profitable at the lower rate. The economy’s MPC is 0.75.

  1. What chapter’s tool explains why a lower interest rate increases the quantity of investment demanded?
  2. What is the multiplier?
  3. Using the multiplier, by how much does this initial $30 billion increase in investment ultimately shift AD (in real GDP terms)?
  4. In the AD-AS model, what happens to the equilibrium price level and equilibrium real GDP as a result of this AD shift, assuming the economy is not already at potential GDP?
Show Answer
  1. The investment demand curve (Chapter 10): a lower real interest rate makes more investment projects profitable, increasing the quantity of investment demanded.
  2. Multiplier \(= 1/(1-0.75) = 4\).
  3. \(\Delta AD = 30 \times 4 = \$120\) billion.
  4. Since AD shifts right along the upward-sloping SRAS curve (Chapter 12), the equilibrium price level rises and equilibrium real GDP rises, this is exactly the chain: Fed action to Chapter 10’s investment demand to Chapter 10/11’s multiplier to Chapter 12’s AD-AS equilibrium.

Problem 3: Cyclical Asymmetry and the Liquidity Trap

  1. During a period of strong demand-pull inflation, the Fed raises interest rates sharply, and borrowing and spending slow noticeably within a few months. Does this illustrate the Fed’s tools working reliably or unreliably?
  2. During a severe recession, the Fed cuts its target rate all the way to nearly zero, but businesses remain too uncertain about the future to borrow, and banks remain too cautious to lend freely. What is this situation called?
  3. In the situation from part (b), what other policy tool (from a previous chapter) might policymakers need to rely on instead?
  4. Explain in one or two sentences why lower interest rates might fail to stimulate borrowing and lending during a severe downturn, even though the same tool works well to cool an overheating economy.
Show Answer
  1. Reliably. This illustrates the “slowing an expansion” side of cyclical asymmetry, where monetary policy tends to work well.
  2. A liquidity trap.
  3. Fiscal policy (Chapter 13), using government spending and taxes to directly boost aggregate demand when monetary policy alone cannot.
  4. Lower rates only stimulate borrowing and lending if businesses are confident enough to invest and banks are confident enough to lend. During a severe downturn, uncertainty about the future can make both sides unwilling to act even at very low rates, breaking the normal chain from lower rates to more investment spending.
