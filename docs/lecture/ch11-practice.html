Practice Problems

Warm-Up: Quick Concept and Calculation Checks

  1. In the aggregate expenditures model, prices are assumed to be sticky in the short run. When total spending changes, does that show up mainly as a change in real output or a change in prices?
  2. True or False: equilibrium GDP always equals potential (full-employment) GDP.
  3. At a GDP level where planned spending (AE) is greater than actual output, what happens to unplanned inventories, and which direction does that push future production?
  4. At a GDP level where planned spending (AE) is less than actual output, what happens to unplanned inventories, and which direction does that push future production?
  5. If the MPC in an economy is 0.75, what is the simple multiplier?
  6. A country’s aggregate expenditures at full-employment GDP fall $15 billion short of what is needed to reach full-employment GDP. Is this a recessionary or an inflationary expenditure gap?
Show Answer
  1. Real output. Because prices are sticky in the short run, changes in spending are absorbed by changes in how much firms produce, not by changing prices.
  2. False. Equilibrium GDP is simply where planned spending equals output, it can sit above, below, or exactly at potential GDP.
  3. Unplanned inventories fall (firms sell more than they planned to produce), signaling firms to increase production, pushing GDP up toward equilibrium.
  4. Unplanned inventories rise (firms sell less than they planned to produce), signaling firms to decrease production, pushing GDP down toward equilibrium.
  5. \(1/(1-0.75) = 1/0.25 = 4\).
  6. A recessionary expenditure gap (spending falls short at full employment, producing a negative GDP gap).

Problem 1: Building the AE Schedule and Finding Equilibrium

A simple, private, closed economy (no government, no trade) has the consumption schedule \(C = 150 + 0.75 \times GDP\) (in billions of dollars), and planned investment is constant at \(I_g = 50\) billion regardless of the level of GDP.

Real GDP Consumption (C) Investment (\(I_g\)) Aggregate Expenditures (AE)
700 675 50 ?
800 750 50 ?
900 825 50 ?
1000 900 50 ?
  1. Fill in the Aggregate Expenditures column (\(AE = C + I_g\)) for each row.
  2. At which GDP level in the table does AE equal GDP? This is equilibrium GDP.
  3. At GDP \(=1000\), is AE greater than or less than GDP? What does this imply is happening to unplanned inventories and future production at that GDP level?
  4. What is the MPC implied by this consumption schedule, and what is the multiplier?
Show Answer
  1. AE at GDP 700: \(675+50=725\). At 800: \(750+50=800\). At 900: \(825+50=875\). At 1000: \(900+50=950\).
  2. At \(GDP=800\), \(AE=800\), so equilibrium GDP is $800 billion.
  3. At \(GDP=1000\), \(AE=950 < 1000\), spending falls short of output. Unplanned inventories rise, signaling firms to cut production, pulling GDP back down toward 800.
  4. The consumption schedule \(C=150+0.75 \times GDP\) has a slope of \(0.75\), so \(MPC=0.75\), and the multiplier is \(1/(1-0.75) = 4\).

Problem 2: Adding Government Spending and Taxes

Starting from the private economy in Problem 1 (equilibrium GDP \(= \$800\) billion, MPC \(=0.75\)), the government now adds \(G = \$50\) billion in purchases.

  1. By how much does the AE schedule shift at every GDP level?
  2. Using the multiplier from Problem 1(d), by how much does equilibrium GDP change? What is the new equilibrium GDP?
  3. The government now also imposes a lump-sum tax of \(T = \$100\) billion. Since \(MPC=0.75\), consumption falls by \(MPC \times T = 0.75 \times 100 = \$75\) billion at every GDP level. Using the multiplier, what is the tax’s effect on equilibrium GDP by itself?
  4. Combining the effects of \(G\) and \(T\) from parts (b) and (c), what is the new equilibrium GDP?
Show Answer
  1. AE shifts up by the full $50 billion at every GDP level (government purchases add directly to spending).
  2. \(\Delta GDP = \text{multiplier} \times \Delta G = 4 \times 50 = \$200\) billion. New equilibrium GDP (with G only) \(= 800 + 200 = \$1{,}000\) billion.
  3. The tax reduces AE by \(MPC \times T = \$75\) billion at every GDP level. Its effect on equilibrium GDP alone: \(-4 \times 75 = -\$300\) billion.
  4. Combining both effects from the original \(\$800\) billion baseline: \(800 + 200 - 300 = \$700\) billion. Even though \(G\) and \(T\) are different dollar amounts, notice that a tax’s effect is always smaller per dollar than spending’s effect, since only \(MPC \times T\) (not the full \(T\)) ever leaves the spending stream.

Problem 3: Net Exports and a Recessionary Gap

Continuing the same economy from Problem 2 (with both \(G=\$50\) billion and \(T=\$100\) billion in place, equilibrium GDP \(=\$700\) billion), the economy now also has net exports of \(X_n = \$50\) billion.

  1. By how much does equilibrium GDP change due to net exports? What is the new equilibrium GDP?
  2. Suppose this economy’s full-employment (potential) GDP is \(\$1{,}100\) billion. Is there a recessionary or an inflationary expenditure gap?
  3. At full-employment GDP (\(1{,}100\)), aggregate expenditures are only \(\$1{,}050\) billion. What is the size of the expenditure gap (the vertical shortfall between AE and the 45-degree line at full employment)?
  4. Using the multiplier, if the government wanted to close this gap entirely, by how much would it need to increase spending?
Show Answer
  1. \(\Delta GDP = 4 \times 50 = \$200\) billion. New equilibrium GDP \(= 700 + 200 = \$900\) billion.
  2. Equilibrium GDP (\(900\)) is below full-employment GDP (\(1{,}100\)), so this is a recessionary expenditure gap.
  3. At \(GDP=1{,}100\), \(AE=1{,}050\), which is \(\$50\) billion short of the \(1{,}100\) needed. The recessionary expenditure gap is $50 billion.
  4. To close a \(\$50\) billion AE shortfall using a multiplier of \(4\): it would take a \(\$50\) billion increase in spending to shift the whole AE line up by \(50\), which raises equilibrium GDP by \(4 \times 50 = \$200\) billion, exactly closing the gap from \(900\) to the full-employment level of \(1{,}100\).
